1. Conventions

Status of claims. Statements in this paper are of three kinds, and it helps to know which is which. Enforced by script: the escrow exits, the pre-signed payouts, the funding output’s withdrawal rule and the refund timelocks — these hold if the scripts are correct, whatever anyone intends. Designed incentive, to be tested: the deterrence of PoB+L+C, the fee market, the insurance flywheel, the review and penalty rates — these are arguments about what rational participants will do, and the economic simulator and early deployments are what will confirm or refute them. Open engineering: the oracle’s threshold construction and nonce protocol, per-chain test vectors, and signing performance on phones (§16, Appendix B). The paper argues the second kind as strongly as it can, but does not present it as the first.

The specification defines requirements and invariants; the reference implementation selects specific libraries, wire formats, and optimisation parameters.

1.1 Original Contributions

Bitaid is built on proven primitives — Bitcoin scripting, Tor[5] (alerts and gossip), I2P[3] or equivalent high-bandwidth anonymous transport (BCAEN uploads and retrievals), Kademlia DHT, content addressing, commit-reveal voting, submarine swaps, and EIP-1559-style fee adjustment — none of which it invented. The following are original contributions:

Proof of ₿ond + Labor + Coherence — a named Sybil-resistance primitive combining refundable Bitcoin collateral, demonstrated human effort and judgment scored against the canon. The bond is anti-staking: it earns no yield, grants no governance, and carries only downside. The labor is an earned record of irreplaceable human effort across real case history, discounted by temporal seasoning; arbitrators are scored for coherence and responders for ballot accuracy. Produces a Human Labor Ledger: an auditable record of demonstrated coordination inscribed on Bitcoin, secured by inherited Nakamoto consensus without a separate token or consensus mechanism.

Three-tier economic offer — incident deposit, bounty, and outcome reward as simultaneous real-time spot market price signals for passive emergency response. No precedent exists for this structure applied to real-world incident coordination by strangers with no prior relationship.

Bitcoin-anchored state machine — application-layer state computed deterministically from a Bitcoin-anchored event log, inheriting Nakamoto consensus for ordering and finality without running a separate proof of work, validator set, or consensus protocol. Any node replays the anchored event log from BCAEN and arrives at identical state independently. Bitaid does not implement Nakamoto consensus — it inherits it.

Closed arbitrator pool with merit-based entry — the arbitrator pool is closed by default; slots open only on natural turnover or sustained demand signal (72-hour non-zero queue). Entry is decided by coherence competition: applicants cast blind shadow votes on live cases and review canon cases, pay a market-priced non-refundable participation fee to BCAEN archival nodes, and lock a scaled intention bond in escrow. Highest scorer wins. Capital cannot guarantee entry; work determines selection. Governance-free, adversarially robust, self-funding via BCAEN participation fees.

Actuarial insurer reserve with encumbrance tracking — a line of cover for incident deposits with real-time encumbrance propagated over gossip, distinguishing real from potential exposure. Opt-in Insurance Buffer Stack (IBS, §4): one or more insurer buffer slots registered per participant in creation-order priority, extending to responder, arbitrator, and archival bonds — consumed sequentially before personal capital on forfeiture, ring-fenced per participant, carrying zero protocol weight.

BCAEN archival market — decentralised content-addressed storage with two archival classes: Class A (incident footage custody, paid from incident escrow via Elements script on authenticated retrieval) and Class B (Bitcoin-anchored application state records, retained indefinitely by operators, paid via Lightning micropayments from bootstrapping nodes). Payment flows from the same conditional Bitcoin escrow that governs the incident itself; the SHA-256 hash is broadcast over gossip before upload completes, so suppressing a recording after the fact would mean suppressing every copy of its published hash; and every authenticated retrieval serves a dual purpose as both service delivery and evidence integrity verification.

Configurable arbitrator quality market — three independently adjustable panel parameters — coherence threshold (80%–99%), open seat fraction (0–⅓), and minimum arbitrator bond (0.1 BTC–uncapped) — embedded at escrow construction, enabling a tiered premium service market for adjudication quality. The ⅔ labor-qualified invariant is non-configurable; all three settings can only raise the Sybil defence. The bond minimum decays gradually if the panel cannot fill (×0.70 at 144 blocks, ×0.70 at 288 blocks, full default reset at 432 blocks).

Passive retroactive fraud detection — fabricated incident chains are surfaced automatically by statistical dissent across calibration queue reviews at a 40% threshold. No individual submitter required. Forfeiture funds the review panel at normal case rates; remainder subsidises BCAEN redundancy via 90-day drip release.

Event-driven arbitration fee market — dual-trigger: +12.5% per case close when queue > 0, −12.5% per case close when queue = 0, plus +6.25% every 18 blocks (~3h) while queue > 0. Price-out ejection after 36-block grace window removes underfunded cases, preserving queue depth as a clean demand signal. No protocol floor. Priority fee: voluntary express review, both parties, pro-arbitrator, pro-speed.

Cell-scoped labor — responder labor scores recorded per H3 cell, preventing remote-farming attacks without requiring global coordination. Labor earned is labor earned where it was demonstrated.

Bond withdrawal delay — 90-day hold from last case close, extended by any active retroactive review. Ensures malicious arbitrator bonds remain present for forfeiture. Early release path available via an arbitrator-requested audit at normal case fee.

Offer-linked integrity — every operator stakes more than it could gain: a responder accepts only with a personal bond plus layer 0 buffer of at least twice the most it could capture (by default), an arbitrator takes a case only with a bond above everything the verdict can move; every node computes the gates identically from the posted economics.

Labor quota with temporal seasoning — two thirds of every arbitrator panel must hold verified case history; seasoning discounts rapidly accumulated records; blind taking and blind commit-reveal voting leave coordinated blocs no way to choose their panels or follow each other.

Zero-cost-till-need participation model — no subscription, no standing retainer, no membership fee. Costs incurred only when an actual incident occurs. Opens emergency response to the uninsured and under-protected who could never sustain private security or insurance premiums.

2. Bonds

Attack surface codes (C2.1, C4.1, etc.) in this section refer to entries in the Threat Vector Map (Appendix C), which enumerates all identified attack surfaces across 9 categories and their protocol mitigations.

2.1 Bond Types

Every bond in the protocol lives in its own independent conditional escrow. Each uses the same dual-backend architecture: a Taproot output on Liquid (Elements) for standard stakes, or a Taproot output on Bitcoin L1 for institutional-scale capital. Bond types differ in who posts them and what triggers forfeiture, but the escrow architecture is shared across every bond. Taproot is live on both chains; signature-hash, block-clock and confidential-transaction details differ per chain and are treated in §3.3.

Caller bonds — three tiers, each independent:

1. Deposit — the refundable good-faith deposit. Proves the incident is real. Returned intact on honest use, forfeited on confirmed misuse. + IBS opt-in (§4).

2. Bounty — an optional appearance payment compensating responders for showing up and documenting, regardless of outcome. Prices the value of presence. + IBS opt-in (§4).

3. Outcome reward — an optional escrow released on a verified outcome — by agreement among those who attended, or by the arbitrators when the result is disputed. Prices the value of the problem being solved. + IBS opt-in (§4).

Each tier prices a different behaviour: the deposit prices honesty, the bounty prices presence, the reward prices intervention. Responders sort incoming alerts by the full economic package — stake credibility, bounty size, outcome reward, labor credentials — not by queue position.

Responder bond — backs the responder’s skin in the game on every incident attended. Forfeited on confirmed misuse or bad-faith arbitration. Fully reserved; no fractional reserve on the base deposit. + IBS opt-in (§4).

Arbitrator bond — dedicated arbitration deposit, minimum 0.1 BTC. Required to sit on panels. Fined 2× to 4× the case fee on incoherent votes, by how isolated the dissent was, compounding up to 32× for a pattern of errors (§10.4). + IBS opt-in (§4).

Archival bond — posted by BCAEN storage operators. Serves two functions: it is the stake that guarantees data integrity (forfeited on corrupted records), and for base slot holders, it is the availability deposit that guarantees retrieval responsiveness (forfeited on a failure to serve that the availability check of Appendix B.5 confirms). The availability deposit is drawn from the bond at slot registration and is returned on honest completion; once a failure is confirmed it is forfeited and the slot is re-auctioned. Scales linearly with declared capacity. + IBS opt-in (§4).

Direct capital always outweighs insured capital in protocol scoring. All registered insurer buffer slots are fully disclosed on-chain — users are responsible for the stack they contract.

2.2 Entry Floors by Role

Each role has its own entry floor, calibrated to the responsibility it assumes. The floors are deliberately asymmetric.

Caller deposit floor — 5,000 satoshis at genesis. Approximately $0.50 at $10,000 per BTC, rising to $5 at $100,000 per BTC. Low enough that any participant with genuine need can register; high enough that bulk false-alarm campaigns face real cumulative cost. The floor follows a deterministic 20% annual nominal deflation from Bitaid’s application-layer genesis block height — calibrated to roughly offset Bitcoin’s historical appreciation range of 10–30% per year, keeping the fiat-equivalent caller floor approximately stable. Computed independently by every Core from block height alone. No oracle, no governance, no trust required.

Responder bond floor — 0.01 BTC at genesis. Approximately $1,000 at $100,000 per BTC. The responder physically intervenes, uses force where the legal framework of citizen arrest applies, and is exposed to civil and criminal liability as well as BCAEN-documented misuse rulings. This floor closes the attack surfaces in C2.1 (phantom attendance), C2.2 (vote collusion), and C2.4 (excessive-force intervention). Unlike the caller floor, the responder bond carries no deflation schedule. As Bitcoin appreciates, the fiat-equivalent cost rises — passively filtering toward professionalisation on the supply side. Individual responders at genesis are joined over time by insured firms and security companies for whom a larger bond is a normal operating cost. The protocol never needs a governance vote to raise professional standards; Bitcoin appreciation does it automatically.

Arbitrator bond floor — minimum 0.1 BTC, fixed. Ten times the responder floor. Arbitrators decide whether an outcome reward is released when the attending vote is disputed, and every other contested case — their verdict moves the largest sums, and incoherent votes are penalised at 2× to 4× the fee, compounding up to 32× for a pattern. The pool is closed by default; entry is via merit-based coherence competition rather than open-market bond posting — this is the primary defence against capital-flood attacks on arbitrator selection (C4.1). The entry mechanism works in conjunction with the ⅔ labor-qualified quota, probation and retroactive review described in §10.3.

Archival bond floor — 0.005 BTC base, scaling with declared capacity. Half the responder floor at genesis. Bond size sets the node’s floor bid in the slot auction; declared capacity above the minimum scales the bond linearly. Forfeiture triggers on a failure to serve retrieval requests that an availability check confirms (Appendix B.5). This floor closes C6.2 (availability fraud) and C6.5 (slot collusion). Carries no deflation schedule. At genesis, nodes may post signal bonds (0.5 BTC or more, 100× minimum) to demonstrate commitment before archival labor history exists; see §8.6 for the genesis cold-start mechanism.

The ratios are deliberate: caller at 5,000 sat, responder at 0.01 BTC (200×), arbitrator at 0.1 BTC (2,000×), archival at 0.005 BTC (100×). Entry cost tracks the liability the role assumes.

2.3 Offer-Linked Integrity and Stake Above Exposure

For high-value alerts, the protocol makes an explicit pairing: no responder should profit more from cheating on an incident than they stand to lose when cheating is proven. At alert construction, the packet carries recommended minimum bond and labor credentials that every node computes identically from the posted economics. On large bounties, the median bond among attendees cannot be dragged down by floor-minimum identities; the offer size sets the comparison baseline.

Stake above exposure. Offer-linked integrity is one instance of a rule every operator role follows: an operator’s own capital at risk must exceed what its counterparty has at stake with it, so that no cheating is worth attempting. For responders, the default acceptance gate is a personal bond — plus any layer 0 insurer buffer, the only layer exposed to forfeiture (§4) — of at least the offer-linked deterrence factor (2×) times the most that responder could capture from the incident’s escrows: the whole bounty, outcome reward and seized caller deposit, since at acceptance no one knows how the split will fall and any one responder could end up with all of it. What a cheat could actually move is usually a fraction of that, so the real ratio of stake to possible gain is usually far above the factor. Every node computes the gate identically from the posted economics; a caller can raise it but not set it below 1×. It is a default market filter, not a protocol floor: the floors in §2.2 still apply, and the effective market minimum rises above them as offers grow. The arbitrator equivalent is in §10.2.

Insurer-backed integrity. On high-value alerts, the recommended minimum bond and labor credentials increasingly assume an insurer buffer as baseline. The protocol strips the buffer before scoring, but the market does not strip it before responding. A caller posting a large outcome reward with a minimal personal bond and no insurer buffer sends a weaker credibility signal than a caller posting the same reward backed by a substantial insurer buffer — not because the protocol weights the extra capital, but because responders and arbitrators observe the insurer’s clean ratio and price the risk differential. The buffer is the market’s proxy for “someone else with capital at risk has already reviewed this participant.”

3. The Escrow

3.1 Funding, Incident and Bond Outputs

At registration the caller sets aside the deposit, bounty and reward in a funding output that stays the caller’s own until a responder accepts: the wallet withdraws it with an automatic oracle co-signature whenever no alert or case is open against it, so the funds stay liquid but cannot be pulled while an alert is live. When a responder accepts, the caller’s scoped signing key moves it into three incident outputs, one per pot — deposit, reward and bounty (below) — and from that moment the money can leave only three ways: cooperative release (caller and responders sign, incident resolves without dispute); verdict release (the oracle — the arbitrator pool’s threshold Schnorr key, FROST[9] — attests to the final outcome, and the payout for that outcome, pre-signed by the parties at acceptance, completes; §3.3, §16); or a refund, also pre-signed at acceptance, that only becomes valid after a long locktime.

Elements Taproot[15] outputs handle the conditional payment flow on Liquid, and the same structure applies on Bitcoin L1 (§13.4). The reference construction is drafted in Appendix B.1; it is a first draft for review, not a validated specification (§16).

Funding output. Before any incident, the deposit, bounty and reward sit in an output with three leaves: the caller’s wallet key plus the oracle’s co-signature, given automatically whenever the anchored event log shows no alert or case open against the output; the caller’s scoped key, used only to sign the incident transaction when a responder accepts (§3); and the wallet key alone after a 365-day fallback (provisional). The check is made against the anchored log, not the mempool, because every node sees the same log but not the same mempool. A caller therefore cannot withdraw while an alert is live. Nor can a caller obtain a co-signed withdrawal in a quiet moment and hold it for later: before releasing its signature, the oracle writes a withdrawal authorised event for that output to the anchored log, and from then on the output counts as spent. Any alert it backs shows in responders’ apps as unbacked, deterministically from the log, so responders see before setting off that there is no deposit behind it. The order matters — the event first, then the signature — and the oracle then broadcasts the withdrawal itself. Before setting off, a responder’s app also checks that the incident transaction is in the mempool with no competing spend. The only remaining window is gossip delay: in the seconds before the event reaches a responder’s Core, an alert on a just-withdrawn output can still be accepted, and the mempool check catches the competing spend. Withdrawals therefore depend on the oracle being online, bounded by the 365-day fallback; the co-signature itself is free, and serving it is part of pool membership. Callers mostly leave their deposit in place as standing self-insurance, so withdrawals are occasional, and a threshold of members can sign them in batches without the whole pool online. Bonds for responders, arbitrators, archivists and signing delegates use a different output (Appendix B.1): a leaf requiring both the operator’s scoped key and the oracle’s co-signature, so a bond can move only by a withdrawal the oracle approves after the hold, or by a forfeiture the operator pre-signed when it took on the exposure; and a fallback leaf that returns the bond to the operator’s wallet alone after a long absolute timelock (365 days at genesis, provisional), so a bond is never trapped if the oracle disappears.

Incident outputs. The incident transaction at acceptance creates three, one per pot — deposit, reward and bounty — each a MuSig2 aggregate of the caller’s scoped key and the attending responders’ keys, spent only by key path; a pot the offer leaves empty gets no output. Splitting the pots keeps the pre-signed set small: each output needs payouts only for its own endings (§11). Cooperative release: all signers sign one settlement spending all three outputs, as the attending vote produced it; no arbitration, no fee market, no delay — the most common path. Verdict release: each possible outcome has a payout the parties pre-signed as an adaptor signature, locked to the oracle’s attestation point for that outcome; when the oracle attests the final outcome, the matching payout can be completed and broadcast by anyone, and no other can. The oracle — a threshold Schnorr key held by the seasoned arbitrator pool (§13.4, §16) — signs one message per pot: the final outcome for that pot, published only after the last re-roll window closes, under a nonce generated for that pot of that case alone and committed at acceptance. It never signs a payout transaction and cannot create an output; it can at worst attest the wrong outcome among the pre-signed ones, which is what panels, review and restitution exist to catch. This is the path through which asymmetric penalties, forfeiture flows, restitution and appeal subsidies execute. Refund: one transaction spending all three outputs and returning each party’s contribution, pre-signed at acceptance with a locktime of 90 days (provisional: about 129,600 Liquid blocks or 12,960 Bitcoin L1 blocks), so funds are never trapped if the oracle never attests. No oracle key appears in either output: the attestation is the secret that completes an adaptor signature, not a spending key, and forfeiture destinations are fixed in each pre-signed payout, so a verdict cannot redirect funds.

No custodian chooses between these paths: before acceptance the funds are the caller’s, and after it every exit was signed in advance by the parties themselves.

3.2 Acceptance-Time Pre-Signing

Acceptance-time pre-signing. Attending responders are unknown when a deposit is posted, so payouts to them cannot be signed in advance, and at a dispute the losing party will not sign anything. Bitaid therefore signs everything at the one moment when all parties are known and none is disputing: when a responder accepts the alert. At that moment the parties sign:

  • the incident transaction, which moves the escrow into three incident outputs, one for each pot (deposit, reward, bounty), each held jointly by the caller’s signing key and the attending responders’ keys;
  • one payout per possible outcome from that output, each locked to the oracle’s attestation for that outcome, as in a Discreet Log Contract[17];
  • a long refund from all three outputs together, in case the oracle never attests.

Nobody judges anything at signing time: the outcomes are the protocol’s bounded set (§11), and continuous splits such as bounty shares by vote weight are rounded into a fixed number of buckets. Because each pot sits in its own output, each needs payouts only for its own endings, so the counts of the three pots add rather than multiply (§11, From predicates to payouts). The incident transaction is broadcast at once, so that from acceptance on the funds cannot leave except by those three routes; the payouts and refund are held, not broadcast. A normal incident closes cooperatively and the payout set is never used. If a dispute opens, arbitration runs, the oracle attests the final outcome once per pot (§3.3), and whoever benefits completes the matching payout in each. The losing party does not need to sign or even be reachable, and the oracle can only select among payouts the parties signed: it cannot create a route.

Who signs for the caller. The caller is in an emergency and may lose the phone, so the caller’s side is signed with a scoped signing key that the caller’s wallet authorises at registration and that can sign only this escrow’s protocol transactions. Any of three holders may carry it: the phone, the caller’s Core (§7, an always-on server, not necessarily at home), or bonded BCAEN signing delegates for callers who run no server. At registration the wallet also commits a template of the caller-side outputs — the recovery address and anything else owed to the caller — and the oracle refuses to attest any payout set that does not match it, so a holder of the scoped key can add responder keys but cannot redirect the caller’s funds. A delegate’s bond must exceed the value of the escrows it signs for (stake above exposure, §2.3), and delegates earn a small fee. Insurer clients sign for their buffer layers the same way.

Delivery. The signature packages are encrypted to the parties and relayed and held by peers and BCAEN until each side collects them, so a weak connection delays the exchange without breaking it; only the incident transaction is broadcast. Responders who accept within the joining window (§5.2) sign together as one incident set. A responder who accepts later is added automatically: when their device reports a position within the scene radius, the signers’ apps or delegates co-sign a new incident transaction that adds them, with a fresh payout set under a fresh oracle nonce, so nobody on the scene stops to sign; the oracle attests only under the newest nonce, so an earlier set can never complete. The set holds at most five signers. Seats fill by proximity as responders arrive and are then kept until the case closes or the holder withdraws: a responder who leaves the scene in pursuit keeps their seat, and nobody can be pushed out by a closer arrival, real or spoofed; later arrivals fill only empty seats. Each re-sign uses a nonce, which the reserve of Appendix B.3 is sized to absorb, and because the set changes only when a seat is filled or given up, re-signs are few. A responder beyond the five attends as a documenter outside the escrow: their bond’s forfeitures are pre-signed toward the signers when they accept, and re-signed automatically whenever a seat changes hands, so they answer for their conduct on the scene; they cannot be repaid from it. Jamming that cuts position, radio and internet across a scene leaves the set as it was, and what happens next is settled by arbitration on the evidence. A signing delegate that uses the scoped key for anything other than the protocol’s incident transaction is caught by the chain itself — the spend does not match the committed template — and loses its bond, which exceeds what it signs for, to the caller (Appendix B.4).

What remains. If no caller-side holder can sign before acceptance — phone, Core and delegates all unreachable — the deposit is never locked, and that incident proceeds without escrow protection: responders can see this before setting off and decide whether to attend. Every accepted incident costs one on-chain transaction, the incident transaction (cheap on Liquid), and on Bitcoin L1 pre-signed transactions need anchor outputs so their fees can be raised when they are broadcast.

3.3 One Attestation per Case

One attestation per pot. Panels and re-rolls (§10.5) vote by blind commit-reveal and record their results in the anchored log; none of them signs anything that moves funds. The oracle attests once per pot, after the last re-roll window has closed, to the outcome the final tier decided — three messages, released together from one replay of the log. Attesting once matters for safety as well as simplicity: a Schnorr nonce that signs two different messages reveals the private key, so each pot of each case gets its own nonce, generated in advance by the pool in batches and used exactly once. The 48-hour embargo (§9) is therefore simply the period before the oracle attests; there is no earlier attestation to supersede and no race to broadcast. The same threshold key is used on both chains; signing uses a FROST variant robust to absent signers (§13.4), and attestations can be batched per epoch to reduce load.

Scope. Pre-committed outcome transactions guarantee a verdict pays out exactly as stated; they guarantee nothing about whether the verdict itself is honest. A bribed or coerced panel attests falsely with the same ease regardless of execution mechanism. That risk is carried entirely by closed-pool entry via coherence competition (§10.2–10.3), blind commit-reveal voting and asymmetric penalty (§10.4), and 40%-dissent retroactive review by the 100-arbitrator pool (§10.3) — mechanisms operating one layer above execution, on judgment rather than settlement. The outcome set must be bounded and enumerable when the alert is accepted; arbitration scope is already restricted to a small set of verdict buckets — directionality of aggression, contract fulfilment, the standard asymmetric-penalty tiers — so this formalises a structure the protocol’s verdict categories already imply.

3.4 Clock Convention

Clock convention. Two kinds of time appear in this specification. Script timelocks (CLTV and CSV) are evaluated by the chain the output lives on, in that chain’s own blocks: about one minute on Liquid, about ten on Bitcoin L1. They are stated here in wall-clock time with the block count per chain. Protocol windows — the fee-market triggers, the 432-block slot-opening window, bond-minimum decay, the price-out grace window — are counted in Bitcoin block height, the clock of the anchored event log (§7), at about ten minutes per block, and are enforced by every node replaying that log rather than by a script opcode. A Liquid script cannot read Bitcoin’s height, so script-enforced paths do not depend on protocol windows; where the two must line up, the script carries its own chain’s block count for the same wall-clock duration.

3.5 Partitioned Escrow

Partitioned escrow. The deposit is partitioned into two non-commingled portions: a collateral partition (pure forfeiture capital, never consumed by fees, seized in full on misuse) and an operational reserve (funds arbitration fees, archival slot payments, and bounty distributions). The operational reserve is spent first, through the incident outputs’ cooperative close or pre-signed payouts, for service costs; the collateral partition is released only to the caller (honest return), to attending responders (misuse seizure), or to the arbitration resolution escrow (disputed outcome). This separation prevents a Sybil attacker from paying escalating arbitration fees out of their own penalty pool, ensuring the full deterrent remains present for seizure.

Open issue (§16, item 7): each incident output is a key-path output spent once, and neither the panel nor the fee is known at acceptance, so the operational reserve cannot pay arbitration fees “when the panel sits” through the pre-signed set as written.

4. Insurer Buffers

Insurer buffer UTXOs may be registered against two distinct escrow contexts, serving different functions in each. The primary application is payment backing: the insurer posts buffer UTXOs alongside the caller’s bounty and outcome reward UTXOs in the incident escrow, ensuring payment to attending responders and arbitrators is honoured regardless of whether the caller’s personal capital can cover it in full. This is the core insurance function — de-risking the caller’s payment commitments so that credible offers can be posted without the caller holding all capital personally. The secondary application is threshold signalling: the insurer posts a buffer alongside the personal bond UTXO, adding visible staked capital that clears subjective discriminatory relay or acceptance policies operated by other nodes filtering for higher skin in the game. No loss event need occur for this function; the value is credibility, not coverage. In both contexts, input ordering — insurer buffer UTXOs in the lower-numbered positions, the personal or payment UTXO last — is fixed in the incident transaction the parties sign at acceptance (§3); any candidate transaction reordering inputs is invalid by construction. This mechanism is identical on Liquid and Bitcoin L1.

Insurance Buffer Stack (IBS). The single-buffer case above is the depth-1 instance of a general mechanism. A participant may register multiple insurer buffer UTXOs against the same escrow in sequence, forming an Insurance Buffer Stack. Consumption follows creation order, but the cascade rule differs by event type, and the two events differ in kind. A payment default — caller unable to meet bounty or outcome reward obligations — is a draw: the insurer’s voluntarily signed policy executes as written, and consumption cascades through the full stack, layer 0 first, then layer 1, until the obligation is met or the stack is exhausted. A draw is not a penalty. A forfeiture event — a misuse or misconduct ruling that forfeits the underlying stake — is a penalty, and only layer 0 is exposed. Layers 1 and deeper are ring-fenced from forfeiture entirely: they are excluded from that whole category of risk, not merely sheltered above a dollar threshold, which differs from an excess-of-loss layer in conventional reinsurance, where higher layers carry the same kind of risk at a higher attachment point. The rationale is structural: stack depth is achieved through pre-arranged insurer relationships with legitimate clients; consuming it on a forfeiture produces no protocol value while making IBS unsafe to offer at scale. The insurer at layer 0 bears the misjudgement or complicity penalty; deeper insurers bear only the risk they priced — payment default on honest clients. Priority within the stack is determined by creation order: the on-chain sequence of insurer signatures is auditable proof of agreed precedence, requiring no additional protocol field.

What stack depth signals. A visible stack signals how actuarially sound the coverage is — its depth, its redundancy, and how many escrows and clients each buffer is pledged to — not capital at risk: only layer 0 is exposed to forfeiture. A single buffer may back several escrows, layered against the probability that they claim together; that is insurance, a contingent promise with default risk, not a deposit, and it is priced accordingly. Each buffer’s encumbrance count (how many escrows it currently backs) is gossiped with it (§1), and any relay or acceptance filter that reads a stack should read that count with it, so that a buffer pledged to many escrows is not counted at full value in each. Insurers’ clearest signal is price: they can back a proposed fork by offering better conditions on it (§15). A shared layer works as intended: it covers each of its clients until one incident draws on it, after which later incidents fall to the next layer or to the client’s own funds. Responders’ apps check that each buffer is still valid before they set off and show what coverage remains, and client apps weigh buffers by the insurer’s record, the layer’s depth and how many clients it backs.

IBS serves two distinct insurer strategies. In the payment insurance model, an insurer covering participants A, B, C, and D posts one thin payment-backing slot per participant rather than a single pooled buffer. A payment event against A draws only from A’s slot; B, C, and D’s coverage is unaffected. Aggregate insurer exposure is identical, but the correlated-loss surface is eliminated. In the threshold signalling model, a participant stacks buffer UTXOs on their personal bond to clear relay or acceptance filters requiring higher visible skin in the game — the insurer lends credibility rather than coverage, priced accordingly. A single participant may combine both: payment-backing slots in their incident escrows and a signalling stack on their bond, arranged with the same or different insurers, each position priced independently. Replenishment of an exhausted slot is the insurer’s operational decision, off-protocol and not time-constrained.

Cross-chain unification. Enforcing insurer-buffer ordering by the SIGHASH_ALL commitment of the pre-signed incident transaction is identical on Liquid and Bitcoin L1 because both chains implement Schnorr signatures (BIP340[15]) and the same sighash types. This removes any dependency on Liquid-specific introspection opcodes for input ordering. The input-ordering guarantee does not depend on Elements features; they are optimisations (confidential transaction amounts, faster block times), not prerequisites for this security property.

Insurer track record as market signal. Every buffer draw and every buffer forfeiture is recorded on the Bitcoin-anchored event log, and they mean different things. A draw is a policy executing as written; it carries no penalty weight, and frequent draws in a cell indicate thin coverage for that zone, which callers, responders and competing insurers can read and price. Nodes compute an insurer clean_ratio — the proportion of backed identities never forfeited, discounted by temporal seasoning — which counts forfeitures only, and only against the layer 0 insurer, the one exposed to them. This ratio is gossip-propagated and advisory only; the protocol does not weight it in arbitrator selection or bond calculation. However, responder nodes and arbitrator panels may reference it as a soft filter. An insurer with a 99% clean ratio signals rigorous vetting; an insurer with repeated forfeitures sees market preference degrade across all remaining subscribers in real time. A single bad actor poisons the insurer’s entire pool.

Co-responder liability cover. An insurer may also cover the harm its client causes to other parties to a case — a fellow responder or the caller’s side — with a published schedule of amounts by injury class (minor, serious, fatal). It pays as a buffer draw, a policy executing as written, so unlike a forfeiture it can reach deeper layers. The protocol sets no amounts: insurers price them, and responders and callers may filter on them at acceptance — for example, taking a scene only alongside responders who carry at least a given cover.

The buffer stack does not replace the personal deposit. Every participant must post 100% of the protocol floor from their own capital. In the payment-backing model, the IBS extends above the personal payment commitment, covering the gap between personal capital and the offered bounty or outcome reward. In the threshold-signalling model, the IBS stacks above the personal bond, amplifying visible capital without replacing it. Stack depth is unbounded by protocol; the personal floor — bond or payment UTXO — is always the terminal input in the spending transaction.

5. Network Roles

The the four bonded roles, the relaying every node performs, their bonds, their economics, and their operational requirements.

5.1 Caller

The person in danger presses one button. Nothing else is required of them.

At registration the caller commits their incident deposit, configures their optional bounty and outcome reward, calibrates their panic trigger, may tag vulnerabilities of the covered person from a fixed list — advanced age, epilepsy or photosensitivity, pacemaker, hearing aid, pregnancy — which travel with the alert to accepted responders only, in a form drone software can respect automatically (free text may be attached, but only the tags count toward fault, §11), and designates a recovery address — a Bitcoin address to receive the deposit return in the event of incapacitation. The stake is already in place before any incident occurs. The wallet also authorises the scoped signing key that will sign the caller’s side when a responder accepts, commits the template of the caller-side outputs, and chooses who holds the key: the phone, the caller’s Core, or bonded BCAEN signing delegates (§3). After that, the caller’s only action in an emergency is the button. The app also offers a duress PIN: entered when someone forces the caller to cancel, it appears to cancel while raising a silent alert — the standard defence of alarm systems against coerced disarming, and the one that matters most to a holder facing a wrench attack.

The panic trigger activates immediately on press. Pre-trigger buffering — a configurable rolling window of up to 60 seconds — captures the moments before the button is pressed as well as after. A 911 call (or local equivalent) is an opt-in feature, off by default. Regardless of whether a 911-equivalent call was placed, an audio transcript of the incident can be attached to the post-incident report as a supplementary evidence track, stored in BCAEN alongside the video footage, and subject to the same access-consent model.

Feel Unsafe mode. Records a local pre-trigger buffer on the device only — no live stream, no BCAEN upload, no gossip load until full panic. On panic, upload, hash gossip, escrow encumbrance, and responder economics activate together. Registration stake and operator relay thresholds still apply to any path that reaches the network. Misuse rules on confirmed false incidents apply after full alert the same as any caller. Because Feel Unsafe recordings begin locally and are not hash-gossiped until full panic, the chain-of-custody proof is marginally weaker than a full alert. The protocol addresses this through timestamp chaining: the panic-triggered BCAEN upload includes the full local buffer as a contiguous stream with no gaps, and the upload hash commits to the entire recording including the pre-panic segment. A verifier can confirm that the pre-panic footage was recorded continuously and has not been tampered with. For the highest-stakes cases, the requesting party may configure escrow settings to require a full alert path rather than Feel Unsafe.

Impersonal callers: alarm systems and automated monitors. A caller need not be a person. A home security system, a commercial alarm panel, an industrial sensor array, or any automated monitoring device can register as a caller identity and trigger alerts autonomously when its sensors detect intrusion, fire, medical emergency, or any configured threat condition. The protocol mechanics are identical: the device posts an incident deposit, broadcasts an alert packet with GPS and cell coordinates, streams evidence to BCAEN, and funds responder bounties from its escrow.

The difference is the quality of the evidence: an automated caller produces a richer BCAEN report than a human caller typically can — continuous video feed from multiple angles, sensor telemetry (motion, heat, sound, glass-break), and pre-incident footage that may span minutes or hours rather than seconds. This richer evidence enables more targeted responder intervention: a responder reviewing BCAEN footage from a monitored premises sees exactly what triggered the alert, where the threat is located, and whether it is still active before they arrive.

Caller identity is held encrypted locally and never transmitted in plaintext. Full identity disclosure is caller-initiated only: the caller signs a disclosure transaction authorising release to specific parties. For impersonal callers, disclosure is configured at registration by the device operator — property address and contact details are pre-authorised for release to attending responders, since a building cannot choose to disclose mid-incident.

5.2 Responder

The most capable people for this work are already trained. They just have no market to sell into.

The responder physically attends incidents. The role requires a higher minimum bond than the caller, a paired Core daemon, and recording hardware. The responder earns income through the three-tier economic offer and builds a labor record that compounds over time.

The alert card shows the full three-tier economic offer — bond amount as credibility signal, bounty as appearance payment, outcome reward and its condition if configured — alongside pre-trigger footage if available, caller attestation status, and the count of other responders who have already accepted. The outcome reward is displayed prominently as the primary competitive signal for professionals. Passing on any alert carries no penalty.

Auto-accept. A responder may set a standing policy — distance, minimum offer, hours on duty — and the app accepts matching alerts instantly, which also starts the signing exchange of §3 within a network round trip of the alert. An auto-accepted responder who cannot actually go may withdraw within a short release window at no cost; after it, acceptance stands and attendance is judged as usual. The same window is the joining window: every responder who accepts within it joins one incident set and signs together (§3). Accepting signs the responder’s side of the incident transaction, the payout set and the refund; each payout goes to a fresh address derived from the responder’s identity (§14.2).

The responder’s personal bond must be fully reserved — no fractional reserve on the base deposit. An opt-in insurer buffer may supplement it, disclosed to all parties, carrying zero weight in protocol scoring, and seized before personal capital on any forfeiture event. Recording hardware — a paired body camera or drone — connects to the companion app via Bluetooth or USB-C and streams footage directly to BCAEN on incident acceptance. Responders also designate a recovery address, as callers do, for indemnity owed to their heirs.

BCAEN upload and hosting costs for responder-originated evidence are funded initially by the responder’s own capital at upload time. This ensures footage lands in the archival network the moment an incident is accepted, before any dispute can arise about settlement. At case close, these upload costs are reimbursed from the caller’s deposit escrow as part of standard settlement. If the caller successfully disputes the outcome, the upload costs remain the responder’s loss. This aligns incentives precisely: responders only upload material they would stand behind under adversarial review.

Intervention labor vs. documentation labor. A responder who intervenes — a deliberate act directed at the threat or at the person in danger, such as restraining, warning off an aggressor they are able to stop, de-escalating, evacuating or administering aid (§11, C2.3) — earns intervention labor score whether or not the act succeeded; success is recorded beside it and paid by the outcome reward. A responder who attends and documents without confirmed intervention earns documentation labor score from the bounty alone. Both scores are permanent, cell-scoped, and public. The attending vote distinguishes them: intervention history grants outcome voting rights and the intervener bounty share; documentation history grants the documentation bounty share only. The protocol does not ask what species of responder attended; it asks what they have demonstrably done, and weights their future participation accordingly. A bonded drone fleet accumulates documentation labor score by default, and intervention labor score when BCAEN evidence shows it intervened: a strobe aimed at the aggressor counts, and so does a warning the drone is armed to back; lighting the scene for the camera, or a warning with nothing behind it, does not.

Scene channel. Responders who accept the same incident share an end-to-end encrypted scene channel, keyed from the incident set and joined automatically by anyone who accepts later. It is not meant to be the best radio on the scene: firms will talk on their own nets, which will always be technically better than anything a protocol can bundle. It does three narrower jobs. It is the directory of the scene: each unit, its liaison, and the channels the units talk on — the coordinator publishes the shared channel between units and the keys to join it, as a communications plan does for a multi-agency incident. It is the ledger of the calls that decide fault — hold, clear, contact, bang, taser, challenge, the coordinator’s assignments — and of each responder’s live position; these are button events of a few bytes, each acknowledged by every member’s device, so they get through on a weak signal and the record shows who received what. And it is the fallback voice channel for whoever has no other: an independent responder, and above all the caller. The caller is on it by default: they can say where they are and whether they are armed, hear who is approaching, and ask for a hold. A caller’s hold is information, not a command: responders remain judged by whether a threat to a person is still active (§11, C2.4), so protecting someone while the caller shouts to stop is lawful. A real cancellation goes through the cancel flow, where the duress PIN covers coercion (§5.1). On it a responder announces an act that endangers others on the scene — a strobe, a sound device, a taser, entry into a building — and anyone can call a hold; a drone announces its strobe automatically before firing it. Its transcript is hashed to BCAEN like footage, and is evidence of what each party said and could know at the time (§11). Talk on an outside channel counts too, through its recordings: each responder records every channel they plug into — the recording is their own defence — and hashes it to BCAEN with their footage. A call made on an outside channel is an announcement to every unit the directory lists on that channel; the listener’s own recording shows it was received, and a unit that declared itself on a channel but kept no recording of it is taken to have heard what was said there. A call on a channel the other unit was not listed on reached no one.

Scene coordination. Every working standard for shared scenes has some hierarchy, and a scene of strangers from several firms needs one too. The protocol supplies the minimum and leaves the rest to the market. Each attending firm is one unit under its own chain of command; an independent responder is a unit of one. Each unit names a liaison on the scene channel — its team leader, or a dispatcher off the scene — who speaks for it to the other units, so the person under fire is not also the one negotiating with other firms. The liaison of the first unit to accept is the coordinator. When a unit with a higher combined merit weight arrives, the app offers it the role, and the coordinator hands over when the scene allows: coordination does not change hands by itself in the middle of a fight. The coordinator makes three kinds of call, each recorded on the channel: it names the contact, the one responder who gives orders to the subject, so the subject is never given orders that cannot all be obeyed; it assigns positions and sectors on the shared map (“hold the east door”, “take the rear”); and it settles challenges between units. It directs units, not the people inside them, and it commands no one: accepting an alert is accepting the scene’s coordination, and what a call changes is the evidence. Following an assignment protects a responder; acting against one without urgency weighs as an ignored hold, and entering another unit’s declared sector is walking into an announced act (§11.7). The role pays nothing and touches neither the bounty split nor any ballot, so there is nothing to win by holding it; an assignment made to sideline a rival is sabotage.

Calls and identification. The short calls — hold, clear, contact, bang, taser, shots, challenge — are buttons as well as words: the app recognises them, ties each to its receipts, and delivers a hold or a challenge ahead of other traffic. Every shared position carries the accuracy its device reports, and a responder is knowably at a place only to within that accuracy; indoors, where satellite positioning is worst, the map alone makes no position knowable. A challenge — one responder querying an armed stranger — makes every accepted device within range flash and vibrate; a responder who is challenged stops, shows their hands and answers before doing anything else. Another responder’s shot is not itself a threat observed (§11.6, C2.4 Q2): it is grounds to take cover and look, and force follows only what the responder then sees.

The floor, not the standard. These rules are a floor. Insurers carry the cost of every coordination failure in their buffers, so what reduces harm between responders — training, drills, shared calls, positioning that works indoors, headsets that mark allies in view — will be priced into cover, and the units that adopt it will be the units insurers back. The standard will rise with the buffers, as compliance with outcome-priced terms rather than with a regulator. The protocol does not choose among these practices; it records what was said, where everyone was and what each could know, so that the price can follow the conduct.

Scope note: the attending vote governs only how the bounty is split among those present — it has no authority over the caller’s deposit or final dispute resolution, both of which remain subject to standard arbitration if any party contests. Its scope is internal to the responder pool: a coordination game over merit, not a unilateral mechanism to adjudicate between caller and responders.

5.3 Relaying (Every Node)

Every AidCore daemon relays by default. There is no separate relay role, no forwarding fee market, and no protocol-level payment for packet routing. A caller who runs their own Core to avoid connecting to third-party infrastructure is simply a self-sovereign participant. A responder relays. An archival node relays. An arbitrator node relays. All of them relay because that is what AidCore nodes do — it is the baseline cost of network membership, not a monetised service.

The network enforces its own liveness through dead-end scoring. Cores that observe a peer failing to propagate alerts attest to that failure over gossip; accumulated dead-end attestations reduce that peer’s priority in routing tables and eventually lead to exclusion. The thresholds are: dead-end ratio above 0.3 over the most recent 200 attestations triggers deprioritisation; above 0.6 triggers exclusion. Both use a rolling window, so nodes that recover honest propagation behaviour recover their routing position over time. Only one kind of failure counts: a peer that signed a receipt for the alert and then did not forward it. A peer that never acknowledged is marked unreachable, which ages its routing entry but leaves its score untouched, so a network split or regional throttling produces no dead-end reports against the nodes cut off. Exclusion is not permanent either: an excluded node stays on probation, receiving a small share of alerts so that its rolling window can show honest forwarding again. Because relaying nodes post no relay bond, there is nothing to slash — dead-end scoring carries no forfeiture component. What it carries is routing consequence: a node that accumulates dead-end attestations sees reduced network presence, fewer alerts routed through it, and diminished peer connectivity. Nodes that want good routing position maintain honest propagation behaviour. Nodes that do not care about routing position are, by that fact, not useful to the network and the network responds accordingly.

5.4 Arbitrator Node

Arbitrators who lose money for being wrong have skin in the game.

The arbitrator node is an AidCore daemon operator who has posted an arbitrator bond of at least 0.1 BTC. Arbitrator nodes are selected to adjudicate contested incidents, including whether an outcome reward’s condition was met. Eligibility requires passing a retro-testing threshold — at least 80% agreement with final rulings and canon answers across a minimum of 10 prior cases — and a 30-day time-lock prevents rapid re-entry after bond forfeiture.

Arbitrator nodes may specialise in subcourt categories — medical, property, detention — and cases are preferentially routed to matching subcourts. An opt-in insurer buffer may supplement the arbitrator bond, seized before personal capital on forfeiture, carrying zero weight in scoring. The full arbitration mechanism is specified in §10.

Bond withdrawal. 90-day mandatory delay from last case close. During the window the bond remains encumbered and subject to forfeiture if retroactive review validates against any case the arbitrator participated in. Early release via an arbitrator-requested audit at normal case fee. Minimum 30-day floor even if all cases audited and cleared. This is the mechanism that gives retroactive fraud detection real teeth: a malicious arbitrator cannot corrupt a verdict and immediately exit with their capital.

Staleness handling. Maintaining a current anchored event log is mandatory for arbitrator nodes, though not necessarily the full history from genesis. A pruned window covering the operational period is sufficient. Staleness is self-correcting: a lagging arbitrator node that proposes a stale panel composition has its proposal rejected by peers with more current state. The rejection carries a reference to the specific anchored event the lagging node is missing; the node fetches that event, verifies its Bitcoin anchor, updates its state, and resubmits. No bond is forfeited for being temporarily out of sync. Staleness causes failed proposals and brief participation delay, both proportionate and recoverable.

Premium tier eligibility. Arbitrator nodes that accumulate 50+ cases with lifetime coherence of 90%+ and 180+ days seasoning qualify for the upper band of the configurable coherence range. No separate registration required. Nodes with bonds above the 0.1 BTC floor are automatically eligible for cases specifying higher bond minimums. Higher-bond nodes find themselves in smaller but more exclusive eligible pools for premium cases, earning higher market-clearing fees.

Arbitrator node operators who retain the full event log from genesis may register as BCAEN Class B state record nodes, earning Lightning micropayments per chunk served to bootstrapping nodes — a complementary revenue stream on data they already hold at negligible marginal cost.

5.5 Archival Node

The archival node is an AidCore daemon operator who has posted an archival bond and declared storage capacity. Archival nodes operate in two distinct classes. Class A nodes bid for BCAEN storage slots at incident creation, host footage and evidence chunks during the retention window, and serve authenticated retrieval requests on demand. Payment is released from incident escrow via automated Elements script execution on fulfilled retrievals. Bond is forfeited proportionally on failure to serve or on corrupted records. Class B nodes retain the full Bitcoin-anchored application event log from genesis and serve it to bootstrapping nodes for Lightning micropayments; these are typically arbitrator or responder operators who already maintain the event log as part of their operational role.

The storage required scales with incident volume and footage quality. A Raspberry Pi with an attached SSD meets the minimum specification for standard-definition footage. Archival nodes may selectively bid for incident types and quality tiers. An opt-in insurer buffer may supplement the archival bond, seized before personal capital on forfeiture, carrying zero weight in scoring. The full archival mechanism is specified in §8.

6. Labor and the Human Labor Ledger

Labor does two different jobs. For arbitrators it is a security gate: two thirds of every panel must be labor-qualified, new entrants serve a 30-case probation, and labor accrues only from real cases, so faster or automated arbitrators do not accumulate it any faster. For responders it is a market gradient: callers and relays use it as a legible filter (a high-value caller can decline untested responders), and it weights their ballots (§9); what each is paid comes from what they did at the incident. The same record, two uses; the specification keeps them apart where it matters.

Responders answer for accuracy. Responders are scored too, but not for coherence. Their ballots — the protocol’s incident reports — are measured for accuracy: whether their account of who did what, and when, held up when the footage was examined (§9). That is an epistemic test, not a moral one. Arbitrators answer for both: they establish the facts themselves from the footage, treating every ballot as a claim to test rather than a finding to accept, as a judge weighs what each side asserts, and they then judge those facts against the canon. Responders answer only for the facts they reported — and sound judgment depends on facts reported accurately. Both lose influence when they are wrong, and neither can buy it back with capital.

The Human Labor Ledger. Every application event that changes protocol state — a verdict, a bond post, a labor score update, a pool slot opening — is a signed message anchored to a specific Bitcoin transaction or block height; §7 sets out which events are committed on-chain. State is computed deterministically by replaying the ordered event log. Any node given the same anchored event log computes identical state. Lagging nodes receive events out of order, verify each against its Bitcoin anchor, and converge on identical state after the fact — the same way a Bitcoin node in initial block download converges on the canonical UTXO set without coordinating with any peer beforehand.

The result is not merely a bookkeeping convenience. It is the protocol’s core output: an auditable record of demonstrated human coordination inscribed on Bitcoin, secured by inherited Nakamoto consensus. Every case, every ballot-accuracy and coherence score, every temporal seasoning milestone, every bounty earned. The bond prevents spam; the labor prevents Sybil; the ledger makes the whole system legible. No separate blockchain. No separate consensus. No native token.

What the Ledger is not. It is not a reputation system in the conventional sense. The protocol distinguishes between labor — objective demonstrated effort recorded on the Human Labor Ledger (case count, temporal seasoning, bounty earnings, ballot accuracy, and for arbitrators coherence) — and reputation — the emergent market signal (on emergence, see Foundations §10.8) derived from that labor (insurer clean ratios, responder standing, arbitrator track records). The protocol weights labor; the market prices reputation. This separation prevents the feedback loops that corrupt conventional reputation systems, where high reputation begets more reputation independent of actual performance.

Cell-scoped labor. A responder’s labor score is not global. Case count, bounty earnings and temporal seasoning are each recorded per H3 cell. Labor earned in one cell does not automatically transfer weight in a different cell. A responder with extensive labor history in one geographic context begins at the floor weight when operating in a distant cell. This closes the remote-farming attack: a Sybil operation cannot manufacture case history in a deserted cell and exercise that weight in a high-value urban dispute, because the labor does not migrate with the identity. Each case is recorded in the cell containing the alert’s committed location, for every attendee whatever their home cell, so a case counts once. When labor is weighed for a case, it is summed over that cell and its 6 neighbours — the same ring the alert reaches — because a responder’s real work naturally spreads across the cells around where they live and move. Labor from beyond the ring carries no weight, so remote farming stays closed. The per-cell record is kept as it is, and the map of where each identity actually works stays readable to anyone looking for staged patterns.

7. Network Architecture

7.1 Core, App and Discovery

The Bitaid Core is a headless daemon running on a Raspberry Pi, server, or VPS. It maintains all network state: DHT participation, Tor circuits for alerts and gossip, high-bandwidth transport circuits for BCAEN uploads and retrievals, deposit credentials, grid cell registration, alert validation, and all cryptographic operations. It exposes a local API via Tor Hidden Service. The Core never holds wallet keys — the user’s wallet retains them and signs locally. It may hold a scoped signing key for an escrow, which can sign only that escrow’s protocol transactions against the wallet-committed template (§3); that is how a caller’s side gets signed at acceptance even when the phone is gone.

The Bitaid Mobile companion app is a thin client handling only GPS, camera, UI, and notifications. Two registration profiles exist: the caller profile (stake configuration, panic button, Feel Unsafe mode, rating UI) and the responder profile (Core pairing, alert reception, recording hardware configuration). A caller in crisis never encounters responder configuration screens.

Node discovery. Kademlia[4]-based DHT adapted to operate over Tor Hidden Services. Each node has a persistent DHT identity derived from its keypair. Bootstrap nodes are hardcoded but replaceable via configuration. The DHT layer handles node discovery only; alert routing uses the geofenced gossip layer.

Grid cell system. H3[6] hexagonal cells (Uber’s hierarchical spatial indexing system, which partitions the globe into nested hexagons of configurable resolution) at resolution 7 (~5 km² per cell, ~1.4 km edge length). Alerts propagate to the same cell and all 6 adjacent hexagons. In low-density areas (fewer than 5 active Cores in the 7-cell ring), propagation expands to the next ring. Cell membership is deterministic from GPS coordinates.

Geofenced gossip. Alerts propagate only to Cores in the same cell and adjacent cells — bounding propagation to the geographic area where response is possible. Dead-end scoring propagates through the same layer: Cores that observe no onward propagation from a peer attest to that observation. Ratio above 0.3 over 200 attestations triggers deprioritisation; above 0.6 triggers exclusion. Only signed-receipt failures count; unreachable peers are not scored. Rolling window, recoverable through probation.

7.2 The Anchored Event Log

Bitcoin-anchored event log. Every application event is a signed message carrying: version, type, timestamp (Bitcoin block height), payload, sender pubkey, and signature. Gossip messages use CBOR canonical encoding. The message ID is the hash of (sender_pubkey, timestamp, payload_hash). Validation requires: signature verifies, timestamp within drift of local block height, type-specific payload well-formed, sender has unspent bond UTXO. Lagging nodes converge after the fact by fetching missing events and verifying their Bitcoin anchors. Anchor verification defaults to a local full node. Lightweight operators may substitute random P2P peer sampling for block-header verification; named explorers are available as an explicit opt-in fallback.

What is written on-chain. Ordering and finality are inherited only for what is actually committed to a chain. Three classes of event are on-chain, by construction or by commitment:

  • Transactions themselves: bond and deposit postings, escrow funding, insurer buffer registrations, payouts, forfeitures, refunds and withdrawals. These are Bitcoin or Liquid transactions; their existence and order are the chain’s.
  • Verdict commitments: a hash of each panel tier’s roster (the arbitrator pubkeys that took its seats) and its predicate answers (§11), committed in one OP_RETURN per tier — carried by the settlement transaction where there is one, and by a separate commitment transaction for intermediate tiers. The plaintext stays off-chain with the panel and the oracle. The coarse outcome is visible anyway from the payout, but the predicate answers that coherence is scored on are revealed against the hash only when review needs them, so the chain does not publish an answer key for calibration (§10.3). A tier’s commitment is either on-chain or it is not; the oracle signs only against a committed roster (§16).
  • Evidence commitments: the Merkle root of each incident’s chunk hashes at incident close (§8.9).

Everything else — gossip, attending-vote detail, dead-end attestations, fork signals, footage — lives on BCAEN and is verified against these commitments.

How off-chain events are ordered. Two honest nodes that received the same events in different orders must reach the same state. Three rules give that order:

  • The chain decides wherever money moved. If two spends conflict — an incident transaction and a withdrawal of the same funding output — only one confirms, and the confirmed one is the fact the log follows.
  • Off-chain events are ordered by the Bitcoin block height they reference. An event is accepted only if it claims a height within 2 blocks of the receiver’s current height, so no one can backdate an event to win a conflict.
  • Within the same height, events that end or invalidate something come before events that start or rely on it. A withdrawal authorisation, bond exit, forfeiture or ejection is applied before an alert, an acceptance, a seat or a vote at the same height; two events of the same kind are ordered by their hash. A withdrawal and an alert in the same block therefore resolve the safe way: the alert shows as unbacked.

Ordering is half the problem; agreeing on which events exist is the other half, since an event could be released late or only to some nodes. Checkpoints settle it. Every 6 blocks the oracle anchors on-chain the Merkle root of all events it has seen; the checkpoint for a height closes a few blocks later, and events arriving after that are rejected. The first checkpoint anchored for a height is canonical; a second, conflicting one is visible evidence of misbehaviour. A participant whose event is left out can anchor its hash on-chain themselves, and the next checkpoint must include it, so omission is provable rather than silent. A checkpoint is final after 6 confirmations and is re-anchored if a reorganisation drops it. The cost is one anchoring transaction an hour; the dependency is the oracle’s liveness, with the same backstops as attestation. A test replaying 2,000 random event sets in 5 different arrival orders each produced identical state every time. Labor is derived from on-chain payouts. On Liquid, amounts are confidential: Liquid stays agnostic to Bitaid use, and the link between a Liquid output and a Bitaid role and amount is revealed only inside the protocol, to the oracle, by the participant disclosing that output’s blinding data. If that proves unworkable, Liquid is reduced to a payment route onto Bitcoin via Boltz, and bonds and escrows move to Bitcoin L1.

8. Evidence Layer (BCAEN)

8.1 Recording and Upload

Recording and upload. On incident acceptance, the responder’s camera streams footage in real time to the nearest BCAEN ingestion node. The stream is chunked at 256 KB intervals. Each chunk is hashed immediately; the hash is broadcast over gossip before the upload completes. This ordering — hash first, upload second — is the suppression resistance mechanism: any verifier can confirm that the content they retrieve matches the hash that was publicly propagated. Suppression would require compromising every layer of the evidence custody stack simultaneously.

8.2 Archival Labor Score

Archival nodes earn a separate archival labor score distinct from responder and arbitrator labor. It is earned through three activities: successful storage (chunk hash matches on upload, confirming data integrity), successful retrieval (authenticated serving within the protocol SLA), and uptime (continuous availability measured by fulfilled retrieval requests). Class B state-record serving also contributes archival labor score, providing a seedbed for nodes to qualify before incident footage exists. The score is cell-scoped: a node earns retrieval credit only for requests served to the cell where it holds the slot. Temporal seasoning applies: rapidly accumulated storage credit is discounted by min(1, days/90), identical to the responder seasoning function. A node cannot buy archival labor score; it can only be earned through demonstrated technical reliability over irreplaceable calendar time.

8.3 Tiered Redundancy: Base Slots and Marginal Slots

BCAEN redundancy is not a flat open market. It is a two-tier system that mirrors the arbitrator pool’s defense architecture:

Base slots (tier 1) — the critical copies. Each evidence package is stored on 3 base slots minimum, up to 5 maximum for high-stakes incidents (configurable by caller at escrow construction, default 3). Base slot eligibility requires a minimum archival labor score. A node with no archival labor history cannot bid for base slots; it can only bid for marginal slots. The base slot threshold is set so that approximately two-thirds of base-slot capacity is held by nodes with substantial archival labor history, with the remaining one-third available to nodes that meet a lower but non-zero threshold. This preserves the same ⅔-labor-qualified invariant that protects the arbitrator pool. Base slots earn higher per-retrieval fees and more stable income; they also carry stricter availability requirements (see §8.5).

Marginal slots (tier 2) — the expansion layer. Beyond the base slots, any bonded archival node may bid for additional redundancy copies. There is no upper bound on marginal slots; more copies are always better. Marginal slots serve two complementary functions. First, they add cost to censorship: a Sybil attacker who floods marginal slots still cannot touch the base copies on labor-proven nodes, and must compromise every marginal copy in addition to every base copy to suppress evidence. Second, they add jurisdictional diversity: marginal slots are held by a broader, more pseudonymous, more geographically distributed set of nodes than the established base-slot holders. A subpoena must reach all copies across all jurisdictions, base and marginal, to suppress evidence. The marginal layer defends against state-level legal pressure; the base layer defends against economic Sybil attacks. They are complementary, not redundant.

Why this closes the Sybil censorship attack. The original vulnerability: an attacker creates many archival identities (bond floor 0.005 BTC, far below the 0.1 BTC arbitrator bond), undercuts all bids for a specific case, wins every slot, and refuses retrieval. The tiered system closes this at both layers. At the base layer, the attacker cannot bid at all: no archival labor score means no base slot access. At the marginal layer, the attacker can win slots but faces two options: serve honestly (contributing real redundancy to the network) or refuse and forfeit bond (losing money while the base copies remain retrievable). There is no profitable path to evidence censorship. The attacker’s best-case outcome is adding more redundancy, which is net positive for the protocol.

8.4 Class A and Class B Revenue

Class A: Incident footage custody. Archival nodes bid for storage slots at incident creation. For base slots, bond size, declared capacity, and archival labor score together determine eligibility and bid priority. For marginal slots, bond size and declared capacity set the floor bid price. Nodes that win slots host footage and evidence chunks during the retention window and serve authenticated retrieval requests on demand. Payment is released from incident escrow via automated Elements script execution on fulfilled retrievals. Bond is forfeited proportionally on failure to serve or on corrupted records. Class A revenue is incident-variable; base slots earn higher per-retrieval fees than marginal slots.

Class B: Application state records. Retains the full Bitcoin-anchored application event log from genesis and serves it to bootstrapping nodes for Lightning micropayments. Class B nodes are typically arbitrator or responder operators who already maintain the event log as part of their operational role. Class B revenue is steady: bootstrapping nodes need the event log continuously, and Lightning micropayments provide regular income for data the operator already holds at negligible marginal cost. Class B serving also earns archival labor score, providing a pre-incident pathway for nodes to build the reputation needed for base slot eligibility. A Class B operator who also runs Class A has their fixed storage costs amortised across both revenue streams and a head start on archival labor accumulation.

8.5 Demand-Driven Availability Enforcement

BCAEN archival nodes hold redundant copies of every uploaded evidence package for the duration of the slot. Availability is enforced by demand, not by periodic cryptographic challenge: any authenticated protocol request — evidence retrieval for arbitration, calibration case review, arbitrator backlog training, or any other routine use — that a node fails to serve triggers bond forfeiture. A failure report alone does not forfeit anything: the reported content is first requested again from independent sources, and forfeiture follows only if those requests confirm the node is not serving it. The check is the availability challenge of Appendix B.5: five probers drawn from different regions, at least four of whom must fail, and a 6-block recovery window before anything is forfeited. Arbitrators also raise challenges themselves when they cannot retrieve content in their normal work. The enforcement is tiered by slot type:

Base slot failure: once the check confirms it, forfeiture of a pre-committed availability deposit (drawn from the archival bond at slot registration) and immediate re-auction of the slot. The availability deposit is sized to cover the emergency compensation to the replacement node plus the re-auction cost at prevailing slot rates — a deterministic function of current fee levels, not a fixed fraction of the bond. A typical deposit might be 3× the current slot fee, calibrated to fund one replacement cycle. This is not a punishment for connectivity issues; it is a pre-funded replacement mechanism. A node that goes offline briefly loses a modest deposit that funds their replacement; a node that deletes or corrupts evidence faces the deeper proportional bond forfeiture. These are two distinct failure modes with different deterrence purposes.

Marginal slot failure: proportional bond forfeiture and slot re-auction within 6 blocks. Marginal slots carry lower availability requirements because they are not the critical path — base copies exist. The 6-block grace window allows for transient failures (network, hardware restart) without disproportionate penalty.

The enforcement primitive is Proof of Bond: the bond at risk structurally exceeds any savings from storage reclamation, making defection irrational regardless of detection probability. For base slots, the availability deposit makes this especially stark: the node pre-commits capital specifically to guarantee availability, and loses it as soon as a failure is confirmed.

Redundancy ensures the protocol is never blocked by a single defector; the defecting node is identified, slashed, and its slot re-auctioned while other nodes fulfil the request. The calibration queue randomly selects from the full case history, creating organic retrieval demand that verifies cold-data integrity as a side effect of arbitration training. Silent deletion is only profitable if every redundant holder coordinates to delete simultaneously — a cartel attack addressed in the threat model. No periodic cryptographic challenge is issued.

8.6 Genesis Cold-Start for Archival Nodes

Base slots require archival labor score, but archival labor score requires serving cases. Who hosts the first cases?

Track A: Signal bonds. At genesis, when no archival labor history exists, nodes may signal seriousness by posting a bond substantially above the 0.005 BTC minimum. A node posting 0.5 BTC or more (100× minimum) signals commitment that cannot be faked: at genesis, the network is unproven, the revenue uncertain, and the capital at real risk. This self-selects for nodes that believe in the network’s future value — the same dynamic that drove early Bitcoin miners to run at a loss. Signal bonds carry no protocol-mandated weight in scoring, but they are publicly visible, and the market (callers, insurers, responder nodes) factors them into slot acceptance decisions. A founding archival node with a 0.5 BTC signal bond and zero labor history may be accepted for base slots on the strength of the bond alone, because the bond demonstrates commitment that would be irrational for a transient attacker.

Track B: Class B serving as labor seedbed. From the moment the first Core daemon boots, Class B traffic exists: bootstrapping nodes need the Bitcoin-anchored event log. Archival nodes that serve Class B requests from day one earn archival labor score from that activity. By the time the first incident fires, a Class B operator may have accumulated sufficient archival labor to qualify for base slots through legitimate pre-incident service. This is the preferred path: labor earned through honest work, not capital posted as a signal.

Both tracks converge: a founding node with a signal bond who also serves Class B traffic has both capital commitment and demonstrated reliability, making it a strong candidate for the first base slots. As the network matures, signal bonds become less relevant and archival labor score dominates, exactly as the coherence competition dominates arbitrator entry over time.

8.7 Responder Self-Backup as Implicit Third Redundancy Layer

Beyond the base and marginal archival slots, every responder who attends an incident retains their own bodycam footage locally. This is not a protocol-designed redundancy; it is an incentive-guaranteed redundancy that emerges from the responder’s own legal defense needs. A responder accused of misconduct needs the footage to defend themselves in arbitration; deleting it would be self-incrimination. The footage persists on the responder’s device regardless of what happens to archival copies.

If all archival copies (base and marginal) were simultaneously compromised, the responder can be contacted via their payment UTXO — the Bitcoin transaction that paid them for attendance. This UTXO is public on-chain; a message can be attached to a small-denomination transaction sent to the same address, requesting re-upload. The responder has both the incentive (they hold the footage for legal defense) and the technical means (they can re-upload to BCAEN from their device) to restore the evidence. No KYC, no contact database, no identity verification is required. The payment trail serves as the contact mechanism.

This creates a three-layer redundancy stack: base slots (labor-gated, 3-5 copies), marginal slots (open market, unlimited copies), and responder self-backup (implicit, incentive-guaranteed, one copy per attending responder). An attacker must compromise all three layers simultaneously to suppress evidence.

8.8 Integrated Archival Economics

Class A and Class B are not independent revenue streams — they form a unified economic system. A Class A-only operator with insufficient slot volume may find operation uneconomical without the Class B subsidy — which is exactly why arbitrator misconduct forfeiture and retroactive chain fraud forfeiture flow to the BCAEN redundancy subsidy pool, subsidising archival capacity that incident-variable revenue alone cannot sustain. The result is a globally distributed evidence layer whose baseline capacity is funded not by a central allocator but by the protocol’s own penalty mechanism redirecting attacker capital into infrastructure that honest participants need.

Note on long-tail evidence economics. BCAEN archival nodes earn revenue from three sources that operate on different time horizons: (1) incident-variable upload and stream fees at incident creation; (2) steady Lightning micropayments from Class B state-record serving; and (3) perpetual retrieval demand from the calibration queue. The calibration queue randomly selects historical cases for new arbitrator training and retroactive review. This creates organic, protocol-mandatory retrieval requests against the full case history, not merely recent incidents. A node that deletes evidence after the retention window expires loses not only the forfeiture risk from active cases but the steady training-traffic revenue from historical cases that arbitrators must review to maintain and enter the pool. The 90-day subsidy drip from forfeiture events is bridge capital, not the terminal funding model. The terminal model is: evidence that has been reviewed once becomes more valuable over time because it enters the calibration canon. BCAEN is not a passive storage market like Filecoin or Arweave. It is an active retrieval market where demand is structurally guaranteed by the arbitration layer’s training requirements.

8.9 Suppression Resistance

At incident close, the final chunk hash is anchored to Bitcoin via an OP_RETURN[19] transaction containing the Merkle root of all chunk hashes. This produces a timestamped, globally replicated commitment that cannot be altered without rewriting the Bitcoin chain from that block forward. To suppress evidence, an attacker must simultaneously compromise all three layers of the redundancy stack: (1) every base slot on labor-proven archival nodes, (2) every marginal slot on the open-market nodes that hold additional copies, and (3) every attending responder’s local device that retains self-backup footage. In addition, the attacker must compromise: (4) the ingestion node receiving the stream at upload time, (5) the Bitcoin chain from the anchor block forward, and (6) the gossip network where chunk hashes were broadcast. Each layer is independent; compromising any subset leaves the remaining layers intact. The security is the conjunction of all six, not the disjunction.

8.10 Feel Unsafe Evidentiary Status

Feel Unsafe recordings begin with a local buffer and are not hash-gossiped until full panic, so the chain-of-custody proof is marginally weaker than a full alert. The protocol addresses this through timestamp chaining: the panic-triggered BCAEN upload includes the full local buffer as a contiguous stream with no gaps, and the upload hash commits to the entire recording including the pre-panic segment. A verifier can confirm that the pre-panic footage was recorded continuously and has not been tampered with between local capture and network upload. This is admissible under the same cryptographic evidence standard as any BCAEN recording, with the caveat that the pre-panic segment has a slightly weaker non-repudiation guarantee because the hash was not gossiped in real time.

9. Settlement by Agreement: the Attending Vote

Each attendee submits a signed ballot weighted by their merit score. The attending vote distinguishes two labor score types already recorded in the Human Labor Ledger:

Intervention labor score — earned from past cases where the responder was found to have intervened (C2.3, §11), with the success ratio of those interventions recorded beside it. It weights the holder’s ballot, including on whether the outcome was achieved; it does not decide their pay for this case.

Documentation labor score — earned from past cases where the responder documented without intervening. It weights the holder’s ballot on the facts of the incident, but not on whether the outcome was achieved; it does not decide their pay for this case.

History weighs ballots; this incident decides pay. Labor scores are a record of past work, and they set how much each attendee’s ballot counts. What each attendee is paid comes from this incident alone: the itemised ballots establish what everyone did, and the contribution weights of C2.3 turn those acts into shares of the intervener and documentation pools. A drone fleet with only documentation history has no say on whether the outcome was achieved, but is paid as an intervener if it intervened here; a first-time intervener with no history votes from the equal-weight floor and is paid for what they did.

intervener_weight = sqrt(stake_ratio) × (0.1 + intervention_labor × bounty_factor)
documenter_weight = sqrt(stake_ratio) × (0.1 + documentation_labor × bounty_factor)

Presence without contribution is not paid. An attendee credited as neither intervener nor documenter — present, but with no qualifying contribution — receives no share of either pool. The other attending responders decide this first, by the same vote that decides the classification; any bonded party may contest it, and escalation to arbitration within the 48-hour window settles it (§11). A genuine attempt that failed still counts as intervention (§11, C2.3); a token gesture earns no intervener share, and only a false claim to credit is penalised, at the bannable tier (§11, C2.1 Q5).

Itemised ballots. A ballot is not a proposed split. It is the protocol’s incident report, but unlike a report written afterwards by one side, it answers fixed questions, is committed blind and signed, points at footage for every claim, and is tested against the video whenever anyone disputes it. It lists every act its author saw — who did it, what it was, when, and a pointer to the footage — and answers the §11 predicates for each: intervention or documentation, its contribution (key, contributing or none), and any harm or obstruction. Ballots are blind: each attendee commits a hash of their ballot within the window and reveals it once commitment closes, as arbitrators do (§10.4), so no one can copy the majority. The split is then computed deterministically. Every attendee’s ballot counts on the facts — which acts happened, and their class and contribution — weighted by its author’s merit weight; whether the outcome was achieved is decided only by attendees with intervention history, as above. The C2.3 rules turn the result into shares. The 80% supermajority applies to each attendee’s classification and contribution rather than to a percentage, which also fits the pre-signed payout buckets. Responders spend the minutes after a case naming what happened rather than bargaining over numbers, and even a self-serving ballot has to point at footage to claim anything.

What arbitrators receive. On escalation the panel receives every ballot, labelled as a party’s claim, with the consensus and the dissent per act, broken down by firm and by role, and a self-interest column: the share each author gets under their own ballot against the share under the consensus. The ballots’ main use is not to reconstruct the incident but to locate who is wrong: it is often easier to find the statement that contradicts the footage, or the claim that is absurd given another party’s account, than to establish every detail of what happened. The protocol lists the contradictions between ballots — the same act described two incompatible ways — so the panel can test those first against the evidence. The ballots do not replace the panel’s own answers: a case reaches arbitration only because someone disputed it, so the panel always sees at least two competing accounts, and arbitrators are scored against the final ruling, not against the responders’ consensus, so adopting a wrong majority still costs them. Each responder’s ballot accuracy — how often their ballots match the final ruling on cases that reach one independently of the ballots, by escalation or by review sampling — is recorded on the Human Labor Ledger and multiplies their ballot weight in both formulas above, from a neutral 1 until five of their ballots have been tested (provisional). Cases closed by agreement are not counted, or a majority would only confirm itself. A responder whose ballots are found wrong loses influence over future splits, and one whose lone dissent is vindicated gains it; no money is taken beyond the shares a wrong ballot would have shifted, and a blatantly false one remains fraud (C2.1 Q5). Credibility used badly goes backwards.

The stake_ratio uses personal capital only, insurer buffer excluded, and is capped at 3× the role’s floor, so a single large bond cannot dominate the vote. The 0.1 floor ensures new responders with no history are not silenced. The square root compression prevents capital dominance while preserving meaningful stake differentiation. The 70/30 split is a protocol default; the caller may configure any split at alert construction, and responders see the split before accepting.

Always disputable to arbitration. The attending vote is the default self-organization among responders at the scene, not a final determination. Any bonded party may escalate any aspect of the attending vote to the arbitration tier within 48 hours: a responder who believes they were wrongly classified as documenter; a responder who catches a competitor drone recording a wall instead of the incident; a dispute over whether a claimed intervention occurred. The arbitrators review BCAEN evidence and issue a verdict via attestation (§13, Appendix B.3) that overrides the attending vote. During the 48-hour embargo, no settling attestation is yet published, and none can be until the arbitration tier has decided (§3.3). If the attending vote produces 80% supermajority and no bonded party files escalation, the outcome reward releases and the case closes. If escalation is filed, the arbitrators inherit the full incident record and resolve the dispute.

Closing the case. An incident output (§3) closes in one of three ways. Cooperative close: every signer signs the settlement the attending vote produced; this is the cheapest path and the common one. Deemed consent: if the attending vote reached its 80% supermajority and nobody filed an escalation within the 48-hour window, the oracle attests the attending vote’s outcome, read deterministically from the anchored log, and the matching pre-signed payout completes; no panel sits and no arbitration fee is charged. If any ballot flags a signer as injured, deemed consent waits for that signer’s ballot or for 7 days (provisional), whichever comes first, and their escalation window runs to the same point, so a responder in hospital is not classified in their absence. If the caller’s key stays silent through the window and the footage shows the caller incapacitated or dead, the classification goes automatically to a 3-arbitrator panel on the same predicates instead of closing on the attendees’ ballots, with the fee borne by the deposit; the one party who could contest the ballots is not absent from the decision. A signer who does not sign — a lost phone, or a holdout hoping to extract a better split — therefore cannot block settlement. If the vote did not reach 80%, the case escalates automatically to a 3-arbitrator panel. Escalation: a bonded party objects within the window and the case goes to arbitration. Arbitration fees are charged to the party whose position the final ruling rejects (§11), so an objection that only delays a settlement the panel then confirms costs the objector the fees.

Dispute frequency and score cementing. Responder-to-responder disputes about classification and bounty splits are expected to be more frequent early in a cell’s lifecycle, when few responders have established intervention labor score and the attending vote produces contested outcomes more often. Each dispute escalated to arbitrators produces a BCAEN-documented precedent that clarifies the boundary between intervention and documentation for future cases. As intervention labor scores stratify and responders internalise the classification from prior rulings, the attending vote increasingly self-organises without escalation. The dispute path remains open permanently, but its expected use declines as the weight distribution cements.

This tier closes a critical attack vector: without it, a caller could refuse to cooperate in closing the case and leave the funds to the 90-day refund, obtaining response services for free. The attending vote lets responders who attended, documented, and acted collectively assert the outcome regardless of caller cooperation. When attendees flag caller misuse — a troll alert, fabricated incident, or bad-faith non-cooperation — and an arbitration panel confirms it (§11, C1.1), the incident deposit is seized and distributed to attending responders pro-rata by merit weight as compensation for wasted presence.

Sybil fast-track via responder report. If responders accept an alert, attend, and find no real incident, they report it as fake through the attending vote. The case is flagged SYBIL_SUSPECT and bypasses the standard fee queue for priority review within 24 blocks. A lightweight 3-arbitrator panel examines BCAEN evidence; if confirmed fake, the caller deposit is seized immediately and the caller identity is banned. Other cases from the same caller pattern (same UTXO cluster, same GPS spoofing signature) are auto-flagged for batch review. The people with the most skin in the game — responders who wasted time and fuel — are the first line of fraud detection.

10. Arbitration

10.1 Scope and Precedence

Attack surface codes in this section refer to entries in the Threat Vector Map (Appendix C).

Arbitrators apply natural law, not legislation. They judge who is the aggressor and who is the victim, and whether the commercial contract was fulfilled. Footage shows directionality — who initiated force, who responded to it — and that directionality is what gets judged, not proportionality measured against a jurisdiction’s use-of-force statute. A responder who restrains a mugger until the caller is safe is not evaluated against a penal code; he is evaluated against whether the aggression he interrupted was real, and whether his presence and documentation fulfilled the outcome the caller paid for. The same applies to a trespasser removed from private property, or a stalker deterred from approaching a caller — the arbitrators determine who initiated the illegitimate aggression and whether the response addressed it, nothing more. Outcome conditions are protocol-set, not caller-written, for a specific reason: a caller who wrote their own conditions could use the protocol to coordinate bounty hunting or manufacture a pretext for harming someone they label an aggressor. The protocol does not permit this. Callers post stakes and bounties. They do not write verdicts.

Bitaid arbitration is a two-tier structure. The first tier — attending-only voting — resolves incident validity and bounty release among parties who were physically present. The second tier — the independent arbitrators — decides every dispute the attending parties cannot resolve, including whether an outcome reward is released, and every finding of misuse or misconduct. Precedence is strict: the attending vote resolves all matters in scope unless any bonded party files escalation within 48 hours, at which point the entire dispute moves to the arbitration tier. Partial escalation is not permitted; the arbitrators inherit the full incident record and issues a single binding verdict across all escrows.

10.2 Arbitrator Eligibility and Selection

Arbitrator nodes must post an arbitrator bond of at least 0.1 BTC. Eligibility requires passing a retro-testing threshold: at least 80% agreement with final rulings and canon answers across a minimum of 10 prior cases. A 30-day time-lock prevents rapid re-entry after bond forfeiture.

Calibration coherence defined. An arbitrator’s calibration coherence score is the share of their cases in which their vote matched the final ruling — after any re-rolls and the review the case may be drawn for — or, on canon cases, the canon’s recorded answers, expressed as a percentage. It measures agreement with the standard, not with whoever sat on the same panel. Cases where the arbitrator abstained do not count toward either numerator or denominator. The score is computed over the arbitrator’s full case history and is not windowed — early incoherent votes persist indefinitely, making coherence genuinely hard to recover once degraded. This is by design: an arbitrator who accumulates a large clean history and then begins voting incoherently sees their score decline slowly, giving the calibration queue time to detect the shift before the arbitrator falls below threshold.

Why 80%, and why 97%. 80% lifetime coherence is the eligibility floor for labor-qualified status. Profitability is set by the penalty: with recurrence-weighted penalties (§10.4) the break-even is about 97% — an arbitrator whose clear disagreements with final rulings exceed about 3 in 100 loses money by continuing to serve — while an arbitrator at 99% keeps over 95% of the income it would earn with no escalation. The threshold functions as a rational-participation filter, and the market can demand more: a party may set the coherence threshold for its own case up to 99%.

Configurable arbitrator quality. Either disputing party may embed three independent quality settings in the escrow at construction time: coherence threshold (default 80%, configurable 80%–99%), open seat fraction (default ⅓, configurable to 0), and minimum arbitrator bond (default 0.1 BTC, configurable upward, uncapped). The ⅔ labor-qualified invariant is non-configurable; all three settings can only raise the Sybil defence. The fee differential above standard is paid ad hoc by the requesting party from their own capital. The bond minimum decays gradually if the panel cannot fill: ×0.70 at 144 blocks, ×0.70 at 288 blocks, full reset at 432 blocks. Coherence threshold and open seat fraction snap to defaults at 432 blocks. These settings create a tiered premium service market for adjudication quality with no precedent in dispute resolution.

Arbitrator stake above exposure. By default an arbitrator is eligible for a case only if their own bond, after any outstanding deductions, exceeds the total value the verdict can move: every escrow in the case, including insurer payment layers a verdict could draw. No party can lower this; either can raise it with the minimum-bond setting above. No single arbitrator can then gain more from a corrupted verdict than they stand to lose, and since a corrupted verdict needs at least two of three seats, a colluding panel puts at least twice the case value at risk to move it once. The bond-minimum decay applies only to the configured premium above this floor and never takes the eligible bond below the case value. Large cases therefore draw from a smaller pool of better-capitalised arbitrators, who earn correspondingly higher fees.

Blind taking. Panels form by taking, not by selection or by opting in to a known case. Eligible arbitrators take seats from the queue as automated takers, seeing only what they need to price the work: the fee, the panel tier, the case value and the escrow’s eligibility filters. Parties, location, incident type and evidence are disclosed only after the seat is committed. When more eligible takers offer than there are seats, seats are drawn by randomness seeded from a Bitcoin block hash mined after offers close, as in Track A (§10.3), so that speed cannot be used to fill a chosen panel. An arbitrator who takes a seat and does not vote is recorded as an abstention on the case and as a decline for inactivity purposes. Taking a seat also means pre-signing, from the arbitrator’s own bond, the forfeiture and restitution payouts to the case’s parties, each locked to the oracle’s attestation; that is what lets a later review repay the harmed party without the arbitrator’s cooperation (§12).

Paid re-rolls. A party who disputes a verdict may pay to re-roll it to a new, larger panel formed the same way (§10.5). Each re-roll costs more than the last but draws from more of the pool, so the weight of honest arbitrators in the outcome rises with every re-roll a party is willing to pay for.

The non-coercibility property. Arbitrator nodes operate as pseudonymous keypairs over Tor Hidden Services. There is no KYC, no registration database, no phone number, no email, and no identity verification. The protocol does not know, and cannot reveal, the physical identity of any arbitrator. This is not merely a privacy feature; it is a security primitive. A nation-state that wishes to coerce arbitrators must first solve the surveillance problem of correlating pseudonymous keypairs to physical persons at scale, across Tor circuits, against targets who may migrate to new keypairs at any time. The coercion cost is additive to the capital-flood cost: surveillance expenditure does not reduce the bond capital required, and bond capital does not reduce the surveillance required. Formal arbitration (§10.9) exists for parties who prefer institutional weight; it is not a security fallback for a weak on-chain system, but a commercial convenience for pre-contracted relationships.

10.3 Pool Closure and Merit-Based Entry

The arbitrator pool is closed to new entrants by default. The pool size target is the rolling median of the active arbitrator count over the last 200 case-event windows. When the current count meets or exceeds the target, no bond can be posted to join. This structural closure is the primary Sybil defence: it is not merely more expensive to attack: capital alone cannot guarantee attack progress, because seats open only on turnover or sustained demand, whatever the attacker can spend.

A slot opens under two conditions. Natural turnover: when an arbitrator voluntarily exits, is ejected for inactivity, or has their bond forfeited, the slot opens immediately. Demand undersupply: when the queue has been continuously non-zero for 432 consecutive blocks (~72 hours) despite fee rises, and all queued cases are verified fundable at the prevailing fee (price-out ejection having removed underfunded cases), the protocol concludes the pool is genuinely undersized and opens one slot. If the queue clears before 432 blocks, no slot opens — the fee mechanism was sufficient.

When a slot opens, entry operates on a three-track system:

Written scenarios in the canon. Alongside real footage cases, the canon can include written scenarios that state what happened and, separately, what each camera captured — the gap between the two is where the hard calls live. The canon holds a fixed set of them, published and changed only by fork. Freshness comes from variants: the same scenario with names, places, participants or camera positions changed, whose answers follow mechanically from the original annotation. A variant never adds a predicate or changes the structure of the episodes; anything that does is a new case. Variants can be generated without limit, so exam and calibration items cannot be memorised, while any genuinely new fact pattern needs a new judgment, and therefore a fork. Friction cases built this way are among the most valuable. They test the application of the predicates, not the perception of events from video, so real footage keeps a share of every exam; and like the rest of the canon they are published and change only by fork.

Track A — Coherence competition (primary track, library ≥ 100 cases). Applicants submit three things, starting within a 48-hour window: (1) a non-refundable participation fee paid directly to BCAEN archival nodes for case retrieval — market-priced by bid/ask; (2) an intention bond of 0.1 BTC scaled upward by applicant count as 0.1 BTC × n^0.3, automatically returned to losers; (3) an exam drawn per applicant from two pools. Live cases are cases currently before a panel: the applicant casts a blind shadow vote that does not count toward the verdict, and the vote is scored against the case’s final outcome after any re-rolls. Canon cases come from the calibration library and are scored against their recorded predicate answers (§11). Live cases carry two thirds of the score and canon cases one third (provisional weights); the number of cases per exam is also provisional: 15 at genesis, 10 live and 5 canon, so that equal per-case weight gives the ⅔ / ⅓ split. Nobody can study for a live case that nobody has decided yet, so live cases test whether an applicant converges with honest judgment on new facts. Canon cases keep that judgment anchored to the non-aggression principle; studying their answers is not cheating, it is the point. The canon share never goes to zero, because live cases measure agreement with the current panel majority, not with the canon. The competition closes when the applicants’ live cases have closed, not at a fixed time, so a slot takes longer to fill than the 48-hour submission window alone. Highest weighted score wins. Live-case scores make exact ties rare; any tie is broken by a draw seeded from a Bitcoin block hash. Case assignment uses per-applicant deterministic randomness seeded by the Bitcoin block hash at height H+6 (a 6-block finality buffer after the submission window closes): each applicant’s live cases are drawn from the open queue and canon cases from the verified library via SHA256(B_H || applicant_pubkey), making assignment unpredictable before the block is mined, verifiable by all participants after the fact, and unique per applicant so colluding entrants cannot share answers. The participation fee is the key anti-Sybil cost: a farm flooding the competition with 1,000 identities pays 1,000 market-priced BCAEN fees, and each identity must independently score well. An attacker who genuinely outscores honest applicants has, in practice, become a calibrated honest arbitrator. The participation fee revenue funds permanent calibration case hosting.

Track B — Invitation (fast path, all phases). A current arbitrator with 50+ cases and 90% lifetime coherence may issue one signed invitation at a time. The invitee skips competition, posts 0.1 BTC directly, and enters the calibration queue. The inviting arbitrator stakes their reputation: if the invitee votes incoherently within their first 30 cases, the inviter’s coherence score takes a proportional penalty. Capital cannot manufacture a legitimate invitation.

Track C — Weighted lottery (genesis fallback, library < 100 cases). Applicants submit a scaled intention bond; one winner is selected by weighted lottery proportional to bond, capped at 3× minimum. A voluntary aged-UTXO signal adds +1 lottery weight per year of confirmed block age on the bond UTXO itself (not a separate attestation), capped at +5. A five-year-old coin used as bond signals voluntary Bitcoin holding through multiple market cycles — conviction that cannot be manufactured on demand. The signal is strictly additive: a fresh coin at 3× minimum bond still outranks an aged coin at floor. Under the planned launch (Appendix B.7) the canon already holds 100 cases, so Track C stays dormant.

Qualification at genesis. The ⅔ labor-qualified invariant cannot be met on day one: labor-qualified status needs 80% coherence over at least 10 prior cases, and at launch nobody has any. Without an exception no panel could form, no case could be decided and nobody could ever qualify. At genesis the canon is the only anchor that exists, so it is the route in: a launch-cohort member counts as labor-qualified after scoring at least 97% on a calibration set of 10 canon cases, compared mechanically with the canon’s recorded annotations rather than with any majority. The score is counted per predicate, not per case: 10 cases give roughly 50 annotated answers, so 97% tolerates a miss, where a per-case count would silently demand a perfect run. The launch cohort itself — about 50 arbitrators selected before launch for alignment with the canon, each still required to pass this test — is, like the canon, part of the protocol’s trusted setup, much as early miners and the first mediators of other peer-to-peer markets were; the network opens for cases only once at least 31 of them have qualified. This route closes once 31 members have qualified through real cases; after that, only real case history qualifies. Until the pool reaches 100, the review panel is the whole qualified pool minus the case’s own arbitrators, however small.

Probation. When a slot opens on sustained queue signal, the winning applicant’s bond is locked with provisional status for their first 30 cases. During probation: their votes are recorded but do not contribute to calibration consensus until the window closes; they occupy at most the open seat fraction of any panel (default ⅓), so even a malicious probationer cannot control a verdict; their participation serves as an additional alert layer for retroactive review, flagging older cases where their dissent from consensus exceeds 40%. A flag does not automatically seize their bond — it sends the case to the 100-arbitrator pool for full review, and that pool must reach 80% supermajority to confirm fraud. Only a confirmed fraud finding results in bond seizure and expulsion. If the 30-case window closes with no confirmed fraud findings, the probationer graduates to normal status. This creates a detention period for queue-pushed entrants without penalising honest randoms, while adding fresh observational capacity to the retroactive review layer.

False alert tolerance. A probationer who flags many legitimate old verdicts for review generates work for the 100-pool without producing value. Each flag that the 100-pool clears as legitimate (fraud not confirmed at 80%) accumulates +1 strike. Each flag that the 100-pool confirms as fraud accumulates −1 strike, rewarding genuine acuity. A net count of 3 strikes during the 30-case probation window triggers bond seizure and expulsion. The rehabilitation offset means a probationer who catches two real frauds can afford four false alerts; one who catches none can afford three before expulsion. Graduation is a separate, stricter test: At graduation, if the probationer’s false alert ratio exceeds 50% (more cleared than confirmed), graduation is denied regardless of net strike count. This constrains noisy probationers to a narrow window while preserving honest disagreement with ambiguous historical verdicts.

What the 40% threshold measures. Dissent is measured at review time, not at vote time. Probationers and calibration-queue reviewers independently assess cases that have already closed, and a case is flagged when dissent among those later reviewers reaches the 40% threshold. A corrupt majority in the original panel produces no dissent inside that panel; it produces dissent among later, independent reviewers, which is exactly the case this layer exists to catch. It holds as long as those later reviewers are not themselves captured, which is why retroactive review is one layer among several (Foundations §6.2) rather than the whole defence.

Review of undisputed cases. Every closed case, disputed or not, is eligible for the calibration queue, and each is drawn for independent review with probability R. R is a published genesis parameter that rises with case value, so larger cases are reviewed more often: 5% for cases up to 0.01 BTC, 10% up to 0.1 BTC, 20% up to 1 BTC and 40% above (provisional, Appendix B.6). The draw is seeded by a Bitcoin block hash mined after the case closes, so no participant can predict which cases will be reviewed. The deterrent compounds across a fraudster’s history: the chance that at least one of n fabricated or corrupted cases is reviewed is 1 − (1 − R)^n, which at R = 5% is 40% after 10 cases, 79% after 30 and 92% after 50. Because every operator’s own stake exceeds what it could gain on a case (§2.3, §10.2), a single act of cheating loses money in expectation once the probability of review passes the ratio of gain to stake — 50% for a responder at the default 2× gate. A history of cheats is not a set of independent bets: once any of its cases is drawn and fraud is confirmed, every case the identities involved took part in within a lookback window is reopened, with restitution from their bonds case by case, and the review rate is raised for a period on cases in the cells where they operated, so accomplices working the same area are examined too (lookback, rate increase and period provisional, §16 item 10). Reviews are funded like calibration review, from Track A participation fees and the arbitrators’ own need to keep their coherence current.

100-pool premium. Arbitrators serving on the 100-arbitrator retroactive review pool earn 3× the normal arbitration fee, but only when a bond was seized in that review. The premium is funded exclusively from seized bonds: when the 100-pool confirms fraud at 80% supermajority, the forfeited bond pays the 3× premium to the 100-pool arbitrators first, then standard panel fees, then the remainder flows to the BCAEN redundancy subsidy pool. When the 100-pool clears a case as legitimate (no fraud), no bond was seized and no premium is available — the arbitrators earn the standard rate. This is a feature: it prevents flooding the 100-pool with trivial reviews and ensures the premium is always backed by actual forfeiture. Arbitrators participate in cleared-case reviews because network integrity protects their sustained fee income from the normal queue, and because the 100-pool convenes infrequently relative to standard work.

Premium feedback loop. The premium creates a direct incentive alignment: confirmed fraud produces seized capital that funds stronger detection of future fraud. The worse the attack, the more seizures, the more premium capital available for retroactive review, the stronger the defense. In low-fraud periods the premium is scarce, but so is the need for it. The mechanism self-tunes to threat intensity without administrative adjustment.

Inactive arbitrator replacement. An arbitrator is flagged inactive when they decline or fail to respond to more than 80% of panel assignments over the most recent 30 case-event windows (minimum 5 cases to avoid false positives). Confirmed by supermajority of peer attestations, the arbitrator’s eligibility is suspended and their slot opens. The bond is not forfeited — inactivity is not misconduct. Replacement only fires when rolling average queue depth over 30 windows is above zero.

The calibration canon

Coherence is anchored twice, and the two anchors are different things. The canon is the launch library of reference cases that fixes what a correct verdict is. It is part of the entry exam — a third of the Track A score, studied openly, since learning the canon’s answers is the point (§10.3) — and it stays in the library afterwards, served in the calibration queue alongside protocol-native cases and scored against its recorded annotation rather than against whoever happens to be sitting on the panel. The final ruling is the second anchor: what the case’s last panel decided, after any re-rolls and any review the case was drawn for (§10.4). It coordinates present judgment; the canon is what stops it from drifting, because a verdict history that departs from the canon is measured against the canon however many arbitrators drift together.

What the canon contains. Real point-of-view footage of police calls and civilian arrests, selected and annotated under the non-aggression principle. It tests conduct in both directions — appropriate and inappropriate conduct by police and by civilians, justified force and gratuitous or punitive force, genuine emergencies and false alarms — because a canon of only clean successes would train arbitrators to approve. Arrests for victimless offences are excluded: there is no aggressor to annotate, so no verdict for the non-aggression principle to anchor. Each case is annotated with predicate answers (§11), not a single holistic verdict, so that it can be back-tested and so that disagreement about the canon is disagreement about a named predicate. Arbitrators are never asked to adjudicate the non-aggression principle in the abstract; they are asked to match the canon’s predicate answers on the case in front of them. That is what keeps judgment from drifting toward rules that no longer optimise for the protection of victims’ lives and property.

Correction. The protocol has no mechanism for amending the canon, by design. A canon error is corrected only by fork — abandonment of the canon and adoption of an alternative — so the canon has to be right from launch. Adoption by participants is what selects whether the canon, and everything built on it, produces value or harm; nothing else in the protocol arbitrates that. The composition of the launch canon and of the expert panel that annotates it are implementation decisions not yet finalised (Foundations §5).

10.4 Blind Voting and Asymmetric Penalty

Arbitrators vote using a commit-reveal scheme: hash(vote, salt, arbitrator_pubkey) is broadcast in the commit phase; the preimage is revealed in the reveal phase. This prevents arbitrators from observing each other’s votes before committing. Late reveals are treated as abstentions — no penalty, no reward.

Asymmetric penalty. Incoherent minority arbitrators forfeit a portion of their bond. The forfeiture amount is the per-case arbitration fee multiplied by a penalty factor ranging from 2× to 4× depending on panel size n and minority proportion p (clamped between 1/n and 1/3):

penalty = 2 + 2 × ((1/3 − p) / (1/3 − 1/n)) capped at 4×

The formula applies to panels of 7 or more. On a 3-arbitrator panel a lone dissenter is a full third of the panel and pays the 2× floor (the formula’s denominator is zero at n = 3). Whether that floor should start higher on 3-arbitrator panels is an open calibration question, since most first-instance work is on panels of 3 and re-rolls carry most of the volume and the best fees.

The bond is the source of funds; the fee is the unit of penalty: the penalty is k × the fee the arbitrator would have earned had the vote been coherent, deducted from the bond. The bond must stay at or above the floor after the deduction; an arbitrator below it must top up before taking further cases. On a case where the arbitration fee is 0.001 BTC, a lone dissenter on a 15-person panel forfeits 0.004 BTC from their 0.1 BTC bond. For a 3-arbitrator panel with one dissenter (p = 1/3): exactly 2× the fee — reasonable disagreement is possible. For a 15-arbitrator panel with a single dissenter (p = 1/15): 4× the fee — a tiny minority against overwhelming consensus, a strong signal of corruption. Voting “insufficient evidence” carries no safe harbour; it is treated as a vote like any other, forcing arbitrators to engage with the evidence rather than abstain strategically.

Recurrence. A single disagreement costs the isolation penalty above. Disagreements that recur close together cost progressively more. Each arbitrator carries a momentum score D: after every case it decays by a factor λ = 0.99 (an error loses half its weight after about 69 cases), and each incoherent vote adds its isolation weight — 1 for a lone dissenter against a large majority, less for a close split, nothing for a predicate the canon marks uncertain. The penalty for an incoherent vote is then the isolation penalty × 2^D, capped at 32×. A scattered, plausible error pays the ordinary 2–4×; a pattern escalates long before review would seize a bond, and even the worst single penalty (32× the isolation penalty) is still a fine. D decays per case, not per day, so a bad streak cannot be waited out by going inactive. Probationers carry no momentum; their votes do not count and the strike system covers them. An arbitrator whose D reaches 3 (roughly three clustered errors) has their recent cases drawn for review automatically. All of these values are provisional and need simulation against real case data (Appendix B.6).

Settled against the final ruling. Penalties are provisional until the case’s escalation windows close, its re-rolls finish and any review it is drawn for has ruled. They settle against that final ruling, not against the first panel. When a larger tier or the 100-arbitrator review agrees with an arbitrator who dissented, that arbitrator is vindicated: their penalty is refunded, their momentum for the case is removed, they receive the fee they would have earned, and they take a share (half, provisional) of the penalties the wrong majority now pays. Standing alone and being right is the most profitable outcome the protocol offers. When a higher tier overturns a verdict, the original majority’s isolation is measured against the final tier’s split, not their own panel’s. A challenge bond and a party’s appeal filed in the same window trigger one re-roll: the appellant pays the appeal fee as usual, and the challenger’s bond rides on the same ruling.

The challenge bond. Only the parties can buy a re-roll, and most cases are never drawn for review, so an honest dissenter could otherwise be penalised with no way to object. A dissenting arbitrator may therefore post a challenge bond (3× the case fee, provisional) to send the case to a larger panel at their own risk. Within the escalation window the challenge works as an ordinary re-roll to the next panel size, and if the larger panel agrees with the dissenter the corrected verdict pays out directly. After settlement the challenge sends the case to the 100-arbitrator review, and if the review overturns the verdict the harmed party receives restitution (§12). If the dissenter is vindicated the bond is returned with the vindication payout; if not, it pays the reviewers. The bond keeps challenges rare and informative: only an arbitrator confident of being right will post one. It also gives a single honest arbitrator on a captured panel a direct, paid reason to expose it, and it brings larger panels to cases whose parties could not afford to escalate.

The penalty amplifies whichever majority exists. Like any majority-coordination mechanism — Bitcoin’s 51% included — it cannot by itself tell an honest majority from a captured one: a captured majority would turn the asymmetric penalty against honest dissenters, which is why penalties settle against the final ruling and why dissenters can post a challenge bond. The protection is not the penalty’s design but the cost, time and exposure of reaching that majority: seats open only on natural turnover or one per sustained 432-block queue window, entry is by coherence competition against the canon, two thirds of every panel must be labor-qualified, and closed cases keep being re-reviewed (§10.3, Foundations §6.2).

Appeal subsidy. The penalty pool does not distribute immediately. It is locked into an appeal escrow. If an appeal is filed, the escrow funds the next round’s arbitration fees — partially or fully — reducing the appellant’s cost. The subsidy is zero when the prior tier was unanimous: the appellant bears full cost when the verdict was already uncontested. The subsidy only materialises when there was genuine disagreement, which is exactly when escalation is most legitimate. Incoherent arbitrators subsidise the reconsideration of their own error.

10.5 Appeal Scaling

Appeals are paid re-rolls (§10.2): each forms a new, larger panel by blind taking. Panel sizes run 3 arbitrators at first instance, 7 on first appeal, 15 on second, 31 on third, and doubling beyond (63, 127 …) for as long as a party appeals and pays. There is no fixed final tier. Each tier has a 48-hour escalation window. Appeal fee: market-clearing arbitration fee + 25% base surcharge per tier, paid ad hoc by appellant. Panel fees always paid first at normal case rate; surplus feeds the penalty pool for coherent arbitrators. Every tier decides by the same ⅔ supermajority, so a small minority of a large panel cannot block correction of a lower tier’s verdict. Each re-roll costs more than the last, but draws from a larger share of the pool, diluting any polluted subset; a larger, unpolluted panel is what exposes and purges the arbitrators behind a corrupted smaller one.

Frivolous appeals are self-deterring: a party that lost a unanimous 3-arbitrator verdict receives no subsidy and faces a 7-arbitrator panel whose dissenters — if any — will be penalised more harshly. Genuine recourse is preserved; manufactured escalation is expensive.

10.6 Fee Market

The arbitration fee operates on a dual-trigger adjustment adapted from EIP-1559, extended with a time-based congestion trigger:

Event trigger (each case close): queue > 0 → fee × 1.125; queue = 0 → fee × 0.875
Time trigger (every 18 blocks, ~3h): queue > 0 → fee × 1.0625; queue = 0 → no change
Slot-open trigger: 432 consecutive blocks with queue > 0 → one pool slot opens

The asymmetry is deliberate. During congestion both triggers fire simultaneously; the fee falls only on successful case closes where the queue clears. By 72 hours of continuous congestion the time trigger alone multiplies the fee by about 4.3× (1.0625^24); each case close while the queue is non-zero adds a further 12.5%, so with ten closes in that window the fee is about 14× its starting level. If elevated fees attract enough arbitrator participation to clear the queue, both triggers stop rising and the fee drifts back down. There is no protocol-imposed fee floor.

Fee market robustness. Three properties make the fee market structurally resistant to manipulation. First, it affects only the arbitration tier — emergency coordination (alerts, responder dispatch, BCAEN evidence commitment, bounty payment) continues regardless of arbitration fee levels. A caller in physical danger still receives responders; only dispute resolution experiences delay. Second, sustained manipulation requires exponential capital burn: the dual-trigger compounding produces fee growth that dwarfs any plausible strategic benefit within days. The cost resembles the chessboard rice problem: one grain on the first square, doubled on each subsequent square; by the eighteenth square the pile already exceeds the entire harvest. Third, even at peak congestion — fees several times their base, queue backed up for 72 hours — the protocol still resolves disputes in days, not the months or years of conventional proceedings.

Priority fee. Any party may attach an optional priority fee to their case, paid from the operational reserve to arbitrators upon resolution. The fee affects only queue order; it does not influence arbitrator selection, panel composition, or verdict weighting. A Sybil caller who tips a fake case pays more to attract additional judicial attention to a case that will be reviewed anyway. For legitimate parties, it functions as express review. Both sides may bid independently.

10.7 Price-Out Ejection and Queue Weight

Queue depth is only meaningful if every case can afford resolution at the prevailing fee. A case whose deposit cannot cover the current arbitration fee inflates apparent demand without being serviceable. A Sybil attacker could exploit this by posting many low-deposit cases, letting fees rise past their coverage, and manufacturing queue depth to trigger slot openings without funding real trials.

The price-out mechanism closes this. At each time trigger interval, the protocol evaluates each queued case: if current_fee × min_panel_size > caller_available_deposit, the case enters a 36-block grace window. If the caller tops up, the case stays; if not, it is removed from queue, deposit returned minus incurred costs through the case’s pre-signed refund. The 432-block slot-opening trigger fires only on genuine unresolvable demand. A Sybil attacker manufacturing queue depth must continuously top up deposits as fees rise — by hour 72 at about 4.3× base fee, each case must carry about 4.3× its original deposit or it is ejected.

Responder legitimacy as queue weight. A case’s queue position is weighted not only by fee level but by the aggregate responder legitimacy of attending parties: sum(responder_reputation × responder_stake) / count. Cases with high-legitimacy professional responder attendance receive priority; cases with no responder acceptance are deprioritised. The market sorts serious incidents above suspected noise without administrative judgment. The queue is two-dimensional: fee level signals caller commitment; responder legitimacy signals incident reality.

10.8 Bond Withdrawal and Exit

An arbitrator initiates withdrawal by broadcasting a signed exit declaration. A mandatory 90-day hold begins from the close of the arbitrator’s most recent case. During the hold: the bond remains encumbered and subject to forfeiture; new case assignments cease immediately; if retroactive review opens against any case, the hold extends for the duration of that review; multiple reviews stack — the bond releases only after all resolve. An arbitrator with a clean history exits at day 90 with full bond returned. An arbitrator who participated in corrupt verdicts faces forfeiture on each confirmed case before any remainder releases.

Early release path. An arbitrator may request retroactive audit of their case history before the 90-day hold expires. Each audit convenes a panel on a specific case; the arbitrator pays the panel fee. If the panel clears the case, that case exits the hold. If all cases are cleared before day 90, the hold releases early, subject to a minimum 30-day floor. This lets legitimate arbitrators with large histories exit faster by actively demonstrating cleanliness; malicious arbitrators requesting audits of corrupt cases accelerate their own exposure.

The 90-day hold closes the “good reputation hiding collusion” attack. An arbitrator with years of clean scores and two corrupt verdicts faces forfeiture on exactly those two cases. The clean history does not offset the corrupt ones; it merely confirms that the remaining cases are clean. A malicious arbitrator cannot corrupt a verdict and immediately exit with their capital.

10.9 Formal Arbitration Path

For parties in pre-existing commercial relationships — an insurer and subscriber, a security firm and client, a property manager and tenant — a voluntary formal arbitration path adds a federation multisig spending path to the escrow at construction time. The federation consists of credentialed AidCore nodes operated by a recognised arbitration body (ICC, LCIA, UNCITRAL, or equivalent).[20][21] The arbitration body adjudicates off-protocol using the BCAEN evidence as documentary record. This path is only available when all relevant parties share a pre-existing institutional relationship including a recognised arbitration clause.

The federation’s N-of-M pubkeys are embedded as a fourth Taproot spending path at escrow construction, alongside the cooperative, verdict, and timeout paths. The chosen institution adjudicates using the same BCAEN evidence the protocol produced, operating under its own independent international jurisdiction rather than under any state’s domestic courts. This is not a security fallback for a failing on-chain mechanism; it is a commercial convenience for parties who already have a contractual relationship with a preferred arbitration venue. The protocol does not compete with formal arbitration; it offers it as an opt-in for parties who want institutional weight alongside cryptographic enforcement.

Why formal arbitration is not the nation-state defence. The on-chain arbitration mechanism is economically self-defending against state-level attack through exponential cost compounding (see Foundations §6.2). Formal arbitration does not supplement a weak on-chain system; it serves a different clientele entirely — commercial parties with pre-existing contracts and preferred venues. A state actor attempting to corrupt the protocol layer faces costs that exceed any realistic security budget within weeks, while simultaneously subsidising honest arbitrators and archivists with their own attack capital. The defence is not jurisdictional; it is mathematical.

11. Dispute-Type Verdict Checklists

11.1 Special Verdicts

Special verdict, not general verdict. Every mechanism above this point assumes a verdict is available to attest to — the attending vote in §9, the blind commit-reveal in §10.4, the payouts pre-signed at acceptance for each candidate outcome (§3). None of that specifies how a panel, or an attending party, actually arrives at a verdict for a given fact pattern. Two things make this more than a drafting gap. First, acceptance-time pre-signing requires the set of possible outcomes to be bounded and enumerated before voting starts; an open-ended holistic judgment call doesn’t decompose into a bounded set on its own. Second, §10.2’s coherence math assumes arbitrators converge on the same answer often enough that disagreement is informative — which holds far better for a series of narrow factual questions than for one holistic “was this handled properly” judgment. Common-law systems solve the equivalent problem with a special verdict: the panel answers a fixed set of factual questions, and the outcome is a deterministic function of the answers, rather than a general verdict where the panel picks the outcome directly. What follows applies that structure to the dispute types that recur most.

The Atomic Verdict Principle. The unit of verdict is the bonded party — one bond, one verdict instance — not a human body and not a physical asset. A single bonded responder may field more than one asset under one engagement (personnel, a drone, a vehicle, or a combination) and still receives exactly one verdict instance, checked against the aggregate of what that identity fielded; an asset-specific predicate is checked against whichever fielded asset is actually capable of satisfying it, not against presence of any fielded asset. What this does rule out is one verdict trying to cover multiple separately-bonded responders at once, which is exactly where an outcome space the pre-signed payout set can’t absorb would come from. Each separately-bonded party gets an independent instance of the relevant checklist below, evaluated against the same shared BCAEN evidence.

One incident record, many verdicts. Some findings are about relations between parties — which act was key, whose act harmed whom, whether an act defeated another’s intervention — and separate verdicts must not tell separate stories. The panel therefore answers once, in a single incident record with the same structure as the attending ballots (§9): the acts, their timeline and the causal links between them. Each bonded party’s verdict instance is read from that record, so every party is judged on the same facts. Each entry is marked observed, inferred or unresolved, so an inference never reads as an observed fact.

Each checklist below is written once and applies at both tiers described in the section introduction: attending parties apply it to each other through itemised ballots (§9), self-interested but fast; arbitrators apply the identical predicates on escalation, slower but reading the evidence cold. What differs between tiers is who is answering, not the questions. The same predicates annotate the calibration canon (§10.3), which is what makes coherence measurable.

11.2 Rules of Evidence

Every predicate below is answered True, False, Uncertain (the sources cover the moment but leave it ambiguous) or Not captured (no source covers it). Five rules apply to all of them.

  • What no source captured cannot be found true. An Uncertain or Not captured answer counts as unproven. Whoever claims payment needs their presence and action captured; whoever accuses needs the misconduct captured. The last column of each table says which way an unproven answer falls.
  • Weight of evidence. Which evidence weighs most depends on the question: physical corroboration — an object, an injury, a second knife on the floor — for injuries and objects; synchronised recordings for chronology; signed channel records and receipts for whether a message was sent or acknowledged. Words recorded during the event, such as a responder shouting a warning, count as evidence of what the speaker perceived. Statements made afterwards weigh least and never establish a predicate alone. An arbitrator who answers Uncertain is counted as giving the predicate’s default (the last column of each table), so expressing genuine doubt is always safe. Each default and presumption applies only to the predicate and checklist that defines it: a presumption favouring a responder on a question of force never establishes a payment, attendance or contribution predicate.
  • Outside sources. Footage from outside Bitaid, such as CCTV or a bystander’s phone, is admissible if it is uploaded to BCAEN within the escalation window with its origin stated. Like other people’s words caught on a recording (“he started it”), it can support a finding but cannot establish one alone unless at least one Bitaid source is consistent with it.
  • Perception at the moment. Wherever a predicate asks what someone believed or perceived — a caller’s alarm, a responder’s reading of a threat — it is judged by what was observable to that person at that moment, including from positions the cameras did not share, not by the full record a reviewer has afterwards.
  • Findings about non-parties. The verdict records findings about people outside the protocol — for example, that a restrained person was not the aggressor — even though the protocol pays them nothing, so that the record can support their ordinary legal claims.

11.3 Caller Misuse — Troll, False Alert or Staged Incident (C1.1)

Not every alert carries a stated incident category — some are a bare location and a distress signal. Q1a exists for that reason: it doesn’t itself determine misuse, it determines what standard Q1b is checked against.

# Predicate Evidence source If unproven
Q1a Alert specified an incident category, vs. a bare location/distress signal Alert metadata at transmission Always captured
Q1b A genuine incident existed at the location and time, judged against the stated category (or any qualifying emergency if Q1a=False) as it was observable to the caller BCAEN footage, pre-alert buffer, responder recordings True — the accuser’s burden
Q2 Caller had a plausible good-faith basis for believing an emergency existed, even if none is ultimately confirmed Alert metadata, situational context in footage True
Q3 Caller has a prior confirmed misuse rating On-chain record — pattern evidence only, never case-decisive alone —
Q4 The caller initiated the aggression they then called protection from Pre-alert buffer, responder footage, admissible outside sources False
Q5 The incident was staged: coordination between the caller and the “aggressor”, or conduct inconsistent with a real emergency Footage, including the aftermath False

Outcomes. Q5=True: staged incident — every colluder (the caller, any bonded “aggressor”, colluding responders) loses deposit and bonds at the bannable tier, all labor credit from the case is voided along with anything derived from it (queue weight, eligibility, invitations), and honest attendees are paid from the seizure. Q4=True: caller misuse — the deposit is seized and paid to attending responders by merit weight; responders who acted in good faith keep their bounty shares, and the outcome reward still pays if its condition was met. Q1b=False and Q2=False: troll — the caller forfeits the whole offer: the deposit is seized the same way, the bounty is paid, and the outcome reward is paid as if its condition had been met, in the bounty’s proportions, so the responders the alert wasted take everything the caller put up. Like every fine, the forfeit reaches no further than the first insurer layer behind the caller; deeper layers, and anything third parties added to the offer, are returned to whoever put them up. These are endings each pot already has, so a troll adds nothing to the pre-signed set. Q1b=False and Q2=True: good-faith error, no forfeiture, no misuse rating: the deposit is returned, the bounty is paid and the outcome reward is withheld, as when a service is no longer necessary on arrival. Otherwise: legitimate, standard settlement.

Who may decide misuse. Attending responders receive a seized deposit, so they may flag misuse but not decide it: every misuse, troll or staging finding is made by an arbitration panel. A misuse finding is recorded against the key that sent the alert: its holder carries the financial consequence, as with any Bitcoin key, but the record does not claim that the holder personally sent it. The §9 SYBIL_SUSPECT fast-track (3-arbitrator review within 24 blocks) remains the expedited path when responders report no real incident.

11.4 Responder Classification and Bounty Split (C2.3)

This is what §9’s attending vote exists to decide, made explicit. Intervention and documentation labor scores are historical — already on the Human Labor Ledger before this incident — and they set each attendee’s voting weight, not their classification or pay for this case. What the vote (or, on escalation, the arbitrators) determines is who did what at this incident.

What counts as intervention. An intervention is a deliberate act directed at the threat, or at the person in danger, meant to change what happens. It is classified by the act’s function and how it was carried out, not by whether it worked and not by what it risked. The caller is buying the threat stopped: a drone that ends an assault with a strobe from twenty metres has done the same service as a guard who steps in, more cheaply and more safely. Paying for risk would pay for the provider’s cost rather than the caller’s benefit, and would penalise better tools. Success is paid separately, through the outcome reward. Presence and recording deter too — that is half of what a drone is for — but they remain documentation: an act counts as intervention only when it is aimed at the threat and goes beyond what documenting needs. Lighting the scene for the camera is documentation, and so is a loudspeaker warning from a drone with nothing to back it: that is presence, which deters without intervening. A strobe aimed into an attacker’s eyes acts on him directly and is intervention. A warning or command counts only when whoever gives it can carry it out — a guard within reach, or a drone armed with a taser or irritant.

11.7 Harm to Fellow Responders — Scene Coordination

Responders at one scene rarely work for the same company, but they share its risks: a strobe that stops an attacker also blinds the guard about to restrain him, a drone flown low obstructs a pursuit, a taser fired across a line of sight hits the wrong person. The protocol does not tell responders how to coordinate; it records whether they did, and penalises harm to each other as C2.4 penalises harm to anyone else. The scene channel (§5.2) is the record. The first question is whether anyone harmed or sabotaged another; how much it matters then depends on intent and urgency. Urgency decides whether coordination was owed at all: an act that could not wait needed no announcement, and one that could wait needed an announcement that actually reached the people it endangered. The same two protections as C2.4 apply — the presumption favours the party who acted, and a finding needs footage or a channel record that makes the failure obvious, not a reviewer’s view that the act could have been better timed.

Reparation is directional, not an indemnity. What it pays is a signal and an internal incentive: the party at fault loses, the party harmed gains, in days, from money already locked, whether or not a state is able or willing to act. Indemnification proper stays with the jurisdiction: the harmed party keeps every legal claim, the record shows what the protocol already paid, and what a court makes of that is the court’s business. Calibrating damages is an unsolved problem outside Bitaid too; Bitaid’s claim is only that its internal incentive points the right way.

11.8 Severity of Responder Penalties

The margin for the hard calls of the field lives in the predicates above — the presumption for the intervener and the blatant standard. Once a finding is made, the penalty follows a short, fixed scale, so that one lapse in a real fight is not treated like cruelty, and every further step remains costly to the person who has already gone too far. For use of force the tier is read from the C2.4 table; the descriptions below summarise it.

Tier What the footage shows Consequence
Lapse A brief excess in or just after a genuine fight — an extra blow in the seconds after the threat was suppressed — with minor or no injury Loses this case’s pay; forfeits 25% of bond and layer 0, taken from the buffer first, escalating with recurrence
Misconduct Repeated or sustained purposeless force, purposeless detention, serious injury from excess, or blatant endangerment of bystanders 100% of bond and layer 0
Bannable Cruelty; force against someone clearly not a threat; fraud — false presence or credit, staging; tampered evidence 100% of bond and layer 0; the identity permanently excluded; labor from the case voided

Recurrence. Like an arbitrator’s momentum (§10.4), lapses compound. Each lapse adds 1 to a lapse score L that decays by 2% per accepted incident (half-life about 35 incidents), and the lapse forfeiture is 25% × 2^(L−1), L counted after adding the new lapse, rounded to the nearest pre-signed fraction: 25% for an isolated lapse, 50% for a second within about 26 incidents of the first, and 100% — the misconduct tier — for a third once L passes about 2.6. A responder gets a margin, never a free pass, and a pattern is treated as what it is.

Harm to other parties to the case. Harm to a fellow responder, or to the caller’s side, is sized by the harm table in the checklist above rather than by these rows, drawn in the same order, and repaid to the harmed party first.

Irreversible findings get the larger panel. Any finding that carries permanent exclusion — the bannable tier, or the cells of the harm table marked excluded — starts at the larger panel used for serious and fatal injury (C2.4) and proceeds up the appeal ladder (§10.5), rather than resting on three arbitrators.

Which assets are taken. Misconduct and bannable findings drain both the personal bond and the layer 0 insurer buffer: the worst case falls on the person who earned it, and an insurer’s layer 0 is its bet on that person’s credibility. Policy exclusions between an insurer and its client are off-chain; the insurer prices that bet and recovers under its own contract. Fees — a responder’s own and those owed to others — and lapse fines are taken from the first insurer layer before the bond, and only in the amount owed: the rest of the layer is returned to an output with the same buffer script and keeps covering its clients. A misconduct or bannable finding takes the whole personal bond and the whole first insurer layer, which for that kind of seizure acts as a larger base deposit. Deeper layers are never touched.

11.11 From Predicates to Payouts

Payouts are pre-signed when the alert is accepted (§3), so every outcome the checklists above can produce must reduce to a small, fixed set before any dispute exists. It does, because each outcome acts on only three things, each held in its own incident output, and on fixed fractions of the parties’ bonds.

  • Deposit: returned to the caller net of any arbitration fees the caller owes, or seized and paid to attending responders by merit weight (caller misuse, C1.1; confirmed non-cooperation, C1.2), or seized and paid first to one responder the caller harmed deliberately or with blatant recklessness (§11).
  • Outcome reward: paid, paid at the pre-agreed good-faith share (outcome not achieved through no fault of the responder, C2.1 Q4), or withheld — one state for the whole incident. When paid, it is split in the same proportions as the bounty vector, so it adds no payouts to the pre-signed set. Callers choose rewards from standard templates, none of which pays for detaining or striking anyone as such — rewards attach to the safety of the covered person or property, so a responder is never paid more for using force than for de-escalating. Each template has an endpoint — handover to emergency services for medical outcomes, property secured from the offender (including when the authorities take custody) for property outcomes, otherwise a fixed time after resolution. A reward is judged from the responder’s arrival to that endpoint: harm done before arrival does not fail it, nothing after the endpoint reopens it, and where there were several aggressors it attaches to protecting the covered person, not to catching everyone. Templates separate a service condition (first aid until handover) from an outcome condition (patient stabilised), and state each in terms the footage can show — compressions visible until handover, not a credential; what the evidence cannot establish stays unproven. Holding an aggressor for handover to the authorities is permitted within the endpoint, and no template pays for it by default, since in some jurisdictions the authorities are no help, or are another risk; a caller who values it can choose a longer endpoint, and a tip can reward it (§13).
  • Bounty split: one share vector over the signing responders, in the buckets of Appendix B.6, computed in a fixed order so that every node derives the same vector. (1) Each bond with credit is placed in one pool (C2.3); a bond with no credit, or not present (C2.1), is in neither. (2) Within each pool, exact shares follow the C2.3 weights. (3) Reductions follow: a bond found at any severity tier (C2.4, or harm to fellow responders) has its share zeroed, and responder fault in the outcome (C2.1 Q4) halves it; the amount removed is redistributed within the same pool to the remaining bonds, in proportion to their weights. A pool left with no eligible bond passes to the other pool, and if both are empty the bounty returns to the caller. (4) The shares are rounded onto the bucket grid by the largest-remainder method — each responder first receives the whole buckets their exact share covers, and the buckets left over go to the largest remainders, ties broken by seat order. (5) The vector must match one payout in the pre-signed set and sum to the bounty. Redistribution is deliberate: it gives responders a stake in policing each other’s conduct, against the habit of teams to cover for a colleague who misbehaves. A complaint without merit gains nothing, costs its author the fees and stays on the record; and because colleagues gain from a finding, they may flag misconduct in their ballots but cannot decide it — a severity-tier finding is made by an arbitration panel, as caller misuse is (C1.1).

Each of the three is held in its own incident output (§3), so each needs pre-signed payouts only for its own endings, and the counts add: at five signing responders in 20% steps, at most 7 for the deposit (returned, seized by merit weight, or seized to one of five harmed responders), 253 for the reward (paid or paid at the good-faith share, under each of the 126 bounty vectors, or withheld) and 127 for the bounty (126 vectors plus the bounty returned whole to the caller) — 387 payouts per incident set, where one shared output would need 7 × 3 × 127 = 2,667, and far fewer with fewer responders or when a pot is empty (no reward offered, no output and no payouts). The reward’s payout must use the same vector as the bounty’s; the oracle attests the three pots together from one replay of the log, so they cannot disagree unless the oracle itself is captured. Everything else acts on bond outputs, each with forfeitures pre-signed when the exposure began (B.1), in fixed fractions rather than amounts set after the fact: 25% and 50% (lapse forfeitures, taken from the buffer first), 100% (misconduct and bannable findings, draining bond and layer 0), and fee reimbursement (a responder whose rejected objection caused the arbitration repays the caller’s fees, from the buffer first). Evidence and access disputes (C6.4, C6.6) act only on archivist and arbitrator bonds, pre-signed at slot registration or seat-taking, and never touch the incident outputs.

Arbitration fees. Fees are paid from the incident output when the panel sits, and charged in the final settlement to the party whose position the ruling rejects: deducted from the caller’s returned deposit, or reimbursed to the caller from the responder’s bond. When the ruling rejects no one — an escalation required by a split attending vote and decided on its merits — fees stay with the deposit, as the cost of the service the caller bought.

Uncertain at the last tier. “Escalate” is not an outcome. When the highest tier a case reaches still cannot resolve a predicate, the predicate’s own default applies (the last column of each table): no one is paid for what they could not show, and no one is penalised for what was not shown. Uncertainty is never a ground for seizure.

Who can be indemnified. Only parties to the case — the caller and the signing responders, whose keys the payouts were signed to — can be paid from an incident output or a forfeiture. Harm a responder causes to someone outside the protocol, such as a bystander or an aggressor struck without justification, is not paid by the protocol; the forfeiture follows the order of §12, and the BCAEN record and the verdict assessment are available to support an ordinary legal claim. Between parties it is different: harm is repaired from the seizure first, as §11 sets out for fellow responders and the caller’s side.

12. Forfeiture

All forfeitures pay out in one order. The type rules below say only who counts as the harmed party and where any remainder goes.

  • 1. Restitution to harmed parties to the case: the caller and signing responders, to keys recorded at acceptance or escalation. For caller misuse, the harmed parties are the attending responders. For harm one party causes another, restitution is the harmed party’s lost pay, then the rest of the seizure — a directional payment, not an assessment of damages (§11).
  • 2. Vindicated dissenters on the case (§10.4): penalty refunded, the case fee, and their share of the wrong majority’s penalties.
  • 3. Review premium: a fixed 3× the case fee to each reviewer, paid only when a review confirmed the misconduct.
  • 4. Panel fees of the deciding tier at the normal rate, where the case’s fees have not already paid them.
  • 5. Remainder to the BCAEN redundancy subsidy pool, drip-released over 90 days, unless the type rule names another recipient.

Why the order matters under capture. Nobody who rules on a forfeiture receives a share proportional to what is seized: reviewers earn a fixed premium, panels their normal fee, and everything above that goes to victims and to BCAEN. A captured majority that falsely condemned an honest participant would gain only fixed fees, not the seized bond, while every such ruling is itself exposed to the challenge bond and later review. Without that limit, a premium paid as a share of seizures would reward an honest pool for catching fraud and a captured one for inventing it.

The type rules:

Caller misuse (troll alerts, a caller who started the aggression, or a staged incident, confirmed by an arbitration panel — attending responders, who receive the deposit, may flag misuse but not decide it): seized deposit distributed to attending responders pro-rata by merit weight; for a troll alert the bounty and the outcome reward are paid out to them as well, so the whole offer is forfeit (§11.3). The stake that induced them to attend on false pretences becomes their payment.

Responder misuse (at the severity tiers of §11 — lapse, misconduct, bannable): the harmed party to the case is repaid first from the seizure — a directional payment, not an assessment of damages (§11); harm to people outside the protocol is left to legal remedies (§11). If no party to the case was harmed, the remainder enters the dispute resolution fund.

Arbitrator misconduct or overturned verdict (corrupt arbitrator expelled by retroactive review at 80% supermajority, or a verdict found incoherent with the canon and majority review): restitution first. The party to the case harmed by the overturned verdict is repaid, from whatever is seized from the arbitrators who voted for it, the difference between what the verdict paid and what the confirmed outcome would have paid, up to the amount seized. Because each arbitrator’s bond exceeds the case value (§10.2), the full bonds of a corrupt two-arbitrator majority cover restitution and the review premium together. Restitution goes only to Bitaid parties to the case, to keys already recorded at escalation; harm to people outside the protocol is left to ordinary legal remedies, which the BCAEN record and the verdict assessment can support. After restitution: vindicated dissenters on the case (§10.4) are refunded and paid; then 100-pool premium at 3×; standard panel fees at normal rate; remainder flows to the BCAEN redundancy subsidy pool (90-day drip release). If the case was flagged by a probationer, the premium covers that review. If no bond was seized (case cleared), no premium is paid and arbitrators earn standard rate. The corrupt arbitrator’s bond does not enrich the panel that ruled on them.

Retroactive chain fraud (fabricated incidents, corrupt verdict chains): any honest party to the fabricated cases is restituted first; then the 100-pool premium at 3× and standard panel fees at normal rate; remainder enters BCAEN redundancy subsidy pool, drip-released over 90 days. Elevated archival node yield attracts new nodes; the attacker’s capital strengthens the evidence infrastructure that detected them.

Archival base-slot failure (archival node holding a base slot fails an availability check, Appendix B.5): forfeiture of the pre-committed availability deposit. 70% of the forfeited deposit is paid to the node that fulfilled the retrieval in place of the failing node (emergency compensation); 30% enters the BCAEN redundancy subsidy pool. The failed node’s base slot is re-auctioned within one block. If the node held marginal slots, those are re-auctioned within 6 blocks with proportional bond forfeiture. This is the tightest forfeiture response in the protocol because base-slot availability is the critical path for evidence survival. The availability deposit handles honest downtime; proportional bond forfeiture handles malicious deletion or corruption. These are distinct failure modes with distinct penalties.

Probationer false-alert seizure (probationer reaches 3 net strikes or fails 50% ratio check): full bond seized. The 100-pool premium at 3× is paid from this seized bond for each review the probationer triggered that resulted in confirmed fraud. If a triggered review cleared the original arbitrator (no fraud), that specific review was already paid at standard rate from the general fee pool. Only confirmed-fraud reviews earn the premium. Remainder after all premium and panel payments enters BCAEN redundancy subsidy pool. The false-alerting probationer funds the premium for the arbitrators who confirmed their genuine catches.

Archival failure (failure confirmed by an availability check, Appendix B.5): forfeiture funds replacement slot costs; remainder enters BCAEN pool.

Sponsorship cascade (sponsor deposit seized on invitee misuse): enters the dispute resolution fund, covering appeal subsidies generated by the invitee chain.

No forfeiture event directly enriches the arbitrators who ruled on it beyond their normal per-case fee. This closes the captured-panel attack: a majority that falsely condemns honest participants cannot extract seized bonds as a reward.

Where an Insurance Buffer Stack is present on the affected escrow, only the layer 0 slot is seized alongside the personal stake; layers 1 and deeper are not touched (§4).

13. Payment Rails

Bitaid operates a dual-chain architecture: Liquid for standard stakes and Lightning for micropayments, with Bitcoin L1 for institutional-scale capital. Chain selection is not a design preference; it is a consequence of the payment pattern each role generates. Tips are ordinary payments, usually over Lightning, to a responder’s per-case address after settlement; they touch no escrow.

Tips: the open-ended fourth leg. The three tiers are fixed before anyone moves; tips are not. Once a case is final, the caller — or an insurer, a neighbour, anyone — may send a responder a tip at the responder’s per-case payment address, in appreciation of service beyond the template: holding an aggressor for the police, staying with the victim, going further than the offer asked. Tips are offered only after settlement, so they cannot buy a ballot; they never count as labor, so no one can tip themselves into a work history; and they carry no protocol weight. Rewards from outside the protocol — a state’s reward for information leading to a conviction, a victim’s private reward, an insurer’s recovery bonus — are claimed off-protocol, with the BCAEN record as evidence; the protocol neither depends on them nor expects them.

13.1 Liquid and Elements

Standard stakes — caller deposits below 1 BTC, responder bonds, arbitrator bonds, archival bonds — operate on Liquid, a Bitcoin sidechain with confidential transactions, native asset support, and 1-minute block times. The faster block time matters for arbitration: an arbitration verdict that releases an outcome reward should not require waiting an hour for Bitcoin confirmation. The confidential transaction support matters for privacy: the amount in a bond escrow is not visible to external observers analysing the chain.

13.2 Boltz Submarine Swaps

A Boltz[10] submarine swap backend handles on-the-fly conversion between Bitcoin, Liquid, and Lightning. This is critical for responder and arbitrator UX: a responder who wants to receive payment in Lightning for instant access does not need the caller to post a Lightning invoice. The caller posts the deposit in Liquid (or Bitcoin L1 for large amounts); the responder’s wallet swaps the payout to Lightning once it lands at their per-case address. The responder designates their preferred receiving chain at registration; the protocol handles conversion transparently.

The swap is atomic: either the conversion completes and the responder receives funds on their preferred chain, or it fails and the funds stay at the responder’s receiving address for retry. There is no counterparty risk beyond the swap backend itself, which operates as a market maker with publicly auditable reserves. A compromised swap backend cannot steal the funds — it can only fail to execute the conversion, in which case they remain with the responder, who can retry or route through an alternative backend.

13.3 Lightning

Lightning is used for payment patterns that require high frequency and low value: Class B archival micropayments (per-chunk retrieval fees paid by bootstrapping nodes), third-party Core connection fees (paid by thin clients to full Cores for routing and validation services), and BCAEN retrieval fees (paid by arbitrators and auditors pulling evidence for review). Lightning is not used for: incident escrow (amounts are too large for channel capacity and require on-chain finality), arbitration fees (require conditional release via script, not preimage revelation), or bond posting (requires on-chain locking visible to all nodes for validation).

The separation is deliberate. Lightning excels at micropayments where trustless finality is less critical than speed and cost. On-chain settlement excels at large-value transfers where finality is paramount. Bitaid uses each for what it does best.

13.4 Institutional Path (Bitcoin L1)

Institutional stakes — bonds above 1 BTC, typically posted by security firms, insurers, or high-net-worth individuals — operate on Bitcoin L1 with Taproot outputs. The same funding, incident and bond outputs apply (Appendix B.1), with anchor outputs on every pre-signed transaction so its fee can be raised when it is broadcast. Taproot is required: MuSig2 aggregation, adaptor signatures and the oracle’s threshold signatures all produce BIP340 Schnorr signatures, which SegWit v0 (P2WSH) scripts cannot verify. MuSig2 aggregates N-of-N and serves wherever every signer is known: the incident outputs, signed at acceptance. The oracle’s key is a threshold key (FROST), because its holders are a majority of a changing pool; panels themselves decide by ⅔ supermajority (§10.5).

Every pre-signed transaction commits to its exact inputs and outputs via SIGHASH_ALL, so nothing can be modified after signing — including the order of insurer buffer inputs in the incident transaction, which enforces buffer ordering (§4). The oracle’s key uses the BIP340-compatible FROST variant (draft BIP, bitcoin/bips #2070, adapted from RFC 9591, whose own keys are not BIP340 x-only keys), with identifiable abort — a cheating participant in a signing session is pinpointed and excluded — and preprocessing to separate expensive offline nonce generation from the online signing phase. The oracle co-signs bond withdrawals and forfeitures as ordinary FROST signatures; its outcome attestations use the per-case nonces of Appendix B.3.

The institutional path exists because large bond holders may prefer the settlement finality and auditability of Bitcoin L1 over the convenience of Liquid. The protocol does not preference one chain over the other; both are fully supported and interoperable through Boltz swaps.

14. Privacy and Security

14.1 Caller Privacy

Caller identity is held encrypted locally and never transmitted in plaintext. The alert contains a pseudonymous identifier derived from the deposit UTXO — sufficient to verify bond validity without revealing identity. Full identity disclosure is caller-initiated only: if the caller chooses to disclose to attending responders or to a legal authority, they sign a disclosure transaction. The protocol does not automatically disclose caller identity to any party, including law enforcement.

Location is revealed in two stages. Initial alert propagation carries only the grid cell identifier (a ~5 km² cell). Precise GPS coordinates are embedded in signed alert packets and shared only with responders who have explicitly accepted the alert — never broadcast to the full cell. The companion app never transmits raw GPS coordinates to any server. All location data is routed only over Tor.

Feel Unsafe mode provides the strongest privacy guarantee: recording is local-only with no network activity until full panic. No live stream, no BCAEN upload, no gossip load, no metadata leakage. On panic, the full buffered footage uploads with timestamp chaining that commits the pre-panic segment to the same cryptographic evidence standard as the post-panic footage. There is no KYC, no registration database, no identity verification, no phone number required, no email address collected.

BCAEN privacy properties. Footage uploaded to BCAEN is content-addressed: the retrieval key is the hash of the content itself. There is no index, no search, no directory. An attacker who knows a content hash can retrieve the corresponding footage; an attacker who does not know the hash cannot enumerate stored content. The hash is gossiped at upload time, so all nodes in the propagation cell learn it, but nodes outside the cell do not. This creates a natural geographic privacy boundary: footage is retrievable by anyone with the hash, but the hash is only known to participants in the incident cell. Arbitrators reviewing cases receive the hash through the arbitrator assignment protocol, not through public indexing.

14.2 Responder Privacy

Responder identity follows the same pseudonymity model. The responder’s Core keypair is the only persistent identifier. Earnings are received at a fresh address for each case, derived from the responder’s identity, so outsiders cannot link payouts across cases; the responder proves each payout is theirs by signing with the identity key, and labor is recorded against the pseudonym rather than against addresses. Coinjoining after receipt therefore costs nothing in labor. Nothing here is linked to any real-world identity by the protocol. Responders who choose to build public market standing may voluntarily link their pseudonymous identity to a verifiable credential, but this is never required.

Responders operate pseudonymously but with higher exposure than callers: they physically attend incidents and their body camera footage is archived on BCAEN. The responder’s Core runs on a dedicated device separate from their personal phone. The responder’s bond UTXO is not linked to their identity by any protocol mechanism, but physical attendance creates correlation risk that responders manage through operational discipline.

The critical distinction is between alert-layer anonymity and footage-layer correlation risk. At the alert layer, the responder is a pseudonymous keypair accepting an encrypted alert over Tor — no IP exposure, no identity linkage. At the footage layer, the responder’s body camera captures their presence at a specific location at a specific time, and that footage is hashed, gossiped, and archived. The footage does not identify the responder (no name, no credential, no biometric), but it creates a physical presence record that correlates with the pseudonymous identity that accepted the alert. A responder who accepts many alerts in the same cell builds a physical presence pattern that could theoretically be correlated with other data sources. This is not a protocol vulnerability; it is an inherent property of physical response work. Responders manage it the same way journalists manage source protection: dedicated devices, operational compartmentalisation, and awareness that physical presence is the one layer cryptography cannot protect.

Correlation attack analysis. A state-level adversary with access to cellular tower records, CCTV footage, and financial transaction data could theoretically correlate a responder’s pseudonymous identity with their real identity by matching physical presence patterns. The protocol mitigates this through: (1) pseudonymous keypairs with no linkage to phone numbers or financial accounts, (2) Tor routing obscuring IP addresses, (3) Lightning[7] micropayments creating no on-chain transaction graph, (4) cell-based alert propagation limiting geographic exposure to a seven-cell area roughly 4 km in radius, and (5) the option for responders to use multiple pseudonymous identities across different cells. A responder who rotates pseudonyms every 30 cases and operates in multiple cells produces no single identity with enough physical presence data to correlate. This is operational discipline, not protocol enforcement — the protocol provides the tools; the responder uses them.

14.3 Device Security

Keys live in the participant’s wallet, not in the app. The companion app is not a wallet and holds no keys. It builds alerts, escrows and transactions and requests signatures from whatever wallet the participant uses — a software wallet, a hardware signer, or a custodial or multi-party setup. Most wallets suffice for a caller placing small deposits. Responders, arbitrators and institutions locking meaningful bonds should use a safer setup than a hot wallet; that choice and its cost are theirs, and the protocol neither mandates nor prices it. Device biometrics can gate the app’s own actions, such as creating an alert or approving a signing request, but they protect the app, not the key. Recovery is the wallet’s own backup, held offline by the user; the caller’s recovery key for the timeout-return path is likewise held in the wallet.

The Core daemon never holds wallet keys — all wallet signing occurs in the user’s wallet. The only key a Core may hold is an escrow’s scoped signing key (§3), which can sign nothing outside the wallet-committed template, so a compromised Core cannot redirect funds. The Core maintains network state (DHT, gossip, BCAEN connections) but all transaction signing requires the private key that lives only in the user’s wallet. If the Core is compromised, the attacker learns network topology and gossip contents but cannot spend bonds, cannot create alerts, and cannot produce verdict attestations.

GPS spoofing mitigation. Multiple independent recorders at the same scene with consistent GPS metadata make coordinated spoofing expensive. A single responder could spoof their location, but spoofing the location of three independent responders plus the caller while producing consistent BCAEN footage is a qualitatively harder attack. Hardware attestation — GPS modules with cryptographic signing, tamper-evident body cameras — is a market-driven response. Insurers who require attested hardware for buffer coverage create economic pressure toward adoption without protocol-level enforcement. The protocol does not mandate attestation; the market prices the risk differential between attested and unattested responders. Over time, high-value alerts will increasingly attract attested responders because insurers competing on clean ratio will demand attestation as a condition of buffer coverage, and callers posting large bounties will prefer responders whose hardware is attested.

14.4 Legal Exposure

Bitaid is designed to work regardless of the jurisdiction around it, which is not the same as being exempt from it. The protocol promises no immunity from local law: a responder who detains, strikes or pursues someone answers for it where they stand, and the evidence the protocol keeps can serve their defence or their accuser. Four exposures are worth stating plainly.

  • Intervention law. Citizen detention, self-defence and defence of others differ by jurisdiction. The checklists of §11 judge conduct by the non-aggression principle, not by any statute; a responder who meets them can still face a local charge, and one who fails them can still be acquitted. Responders, and the insurers who back them, carry that difference; insurers backing professional responders can bundle legal-defence cover, as security firms’ insurers already do.
  • Unlawful content. BCAEN stores only uploads tied to a bonded, live incident, which limits abuse but cannot exclude it. Content an arbitration panel finds unlawful is purged from every slot, with its hash kept as the record of the ruling (C6.6) — unlike forged content, which is quarantined and kept as evidence of the forgery (§11) — and an archival node may refuse to host it.
  • Targeting innocent people. A caller can send responders after someone who has done nothing. The caller’s deposit and misuse rating are the deterrent (C1.1); the target’s remedy is an ordinary legal claim, supported by the BCAEN record and the verdict’s findings about non-parties (§11).
  • Capital and insurance. Bonds carry no KYC by design. Insurers that post buffers operate under their own licences and compliance, as a business layer above the protocol, not as part of it.

15. Protocol Evolution

Bitaid has no governance token, no foundation, no committee, and no upgrade authority. Protocol changes propagate through the same mechanism that governs all Bitcoin forks: revealed capital preference — but with a critical difference. Raw capital weight alone does not determine signal weight.

The signal layer. Participants express version preference through metadata attached to normal protocol transactions — bond renewals, escrow openings, BCAEN slot registrations. No vote is called, no quorum convened. Each on-chain act the participant was already performing carries a version signal, and the aggregate of those signals is public, real-time, and unforgeable. Capital cannot lie about where it is. But a whale who has never responded to an incident, never archived evidence, never resolved a dispute carries a signal weighted close to zero regardless of bond size — because signal weight is a product of bond and labor score, the same merit function used everywhere else in the protocol. A participant with no cell-scoped, temporally seasoned participation history has no governance weight to throw around. They would have to earn it the same way they earn everything else.

The temporal seasoning compounds this. Even a well-capitalised participant who decides to start building signal weight cannot accelerate the 90-day seasoning function. Labor history cannot be manufactured on demand. By the time their signal weight is meaningful, they have genuine skin in the outcome — they have been operating honestly in the protocol long enough that destroying it through a bad fork would cost them more than staying honest.

Signal duration is proposal-specific. There is no fixed activation window. An urgent bug fix with clear supermajority requires no extended signalling period — the consensus is self-evident and speed is the priority. A contentious architectural change requires sustained supermajority over a longer community-determined period before nodes begin treating it as the default standard. What matters is that the signal is labor-qualified and durable, not that it meets an arbitrary countdown. Proposals that cannot sustain their signal weight over time were never genuine consensus.

A signal, not a decision. The signal layer is a poll of labor-qualified opinion, filtered so that Sybil noise does not distort it. It has no executing authority: no software switches version because a threshold is crossed, and no participant is obliged to follow it. Each participant chooses which version to run. Phrases such as “sustained supermajority” and “begin treating it as the default” describe what individual participants tend to do when the signal is clear, not a rule that fires; nobody decides what counts as sustained, and each participant decides for themselves. The consequential layer is what follows: responders, insurers and callers move to the version that serves them, or stay on the old one, and a change that does not improve protection or price is simply ignored.

Soft fork path. A change that tightens existing constraints without breaking old transaction validity propagates through economic pressure alone. Nodes running older software still process valid transactions; participants who have not upgraded are not ejected. As supermajority labor-qualified preference accumulates in the signal layer, the economic cost of operating outside the emerging standard rises until holdouts converge or exit. No authority forced them.

Hard fork path. A contentious change produces two coexisting versions of the protocol. Bonded participants — responders, arbitrators, archivists — must choose which escrow environment to operate in. Their bond is committed; switching has a cost. A well-capitalised actor who signals for a fork and migrates to it is not imposing anything on anyone — they are serving themselves in isolation. Nobody can be forced to adopt alternative software without a forced auto-update, which the protocol has no mechanism to deliver. A signal is a signal. Capital weight without labor track record is low signal. If a major market-maker for responders migrates, that is relevant because they are the market — not because of the size of their bond.

How insurers, callers and bonded operators sort a contested fork in practice is argued in Foundations §7.

16. Open Problems

The sections above state what the escrow scripts must achieve. They are not yet a validated script-level specification, and the problems below are open. None is believed unsolvable with existing Bitcoin and Liquid primitives; none is solved by this document.

1. Oracle key at pool scale. The verdict path no longer needs anyone to sign at a dispute: payouts are pre-signed at acceptance (§3) and the oracle only attests. What remains is the oracle’s own key. It is held by a threshold of about a majority of the seasoned pool, each member signing only if a deterministic replay of the anchored log confirms that the outcome follows from the recorded votes and roster. Open questions: distributed key generation and proactive resharing as members join and leave without changing the public key (D-FROST, IACR ePrint 2024/896, is the starting point); signing with hundreds of participants, some absent (a ROAST-style wrapper, and batching attestations per epoch); and common-mode failure, since one client flaw that exposes shares exposes them all, which argues for isolated share storage, frequent refresh and no silent updates. Because the oracle can only choose among pre-signed payouts, a captured majority can push wrong verdicts case by case but cannot sweep open escrows.

2. Per-case nonces. Each case’s attestation nonce must be generated jointly by the pool before the alert is accepted, committed with the case, and used exactly once. Appendix B.3 drafts the construction: batched joint generation each epoch with a reserve well above peak load, refilled as soon as half is used, reshared with the key at epoch boundaries, each nonce used for exactly one message. It needs cryptographic review before any funds depend on it.

3. Availability attestation. BCAEN’s availability forfeiture (§8) requires someone to attest that an authenticated retrieval failed. Appendix B.5 drafts the procedure: a challenge bond, five randomly drawn arbitrators sending ordinary anonymous retrieval requests, forfeiture only if at least four fail to retrieve.

4. Per-chain semantics. Signature hashes, confidential-transaction commitments and block clocks differ between Liquid and Bitcoin L1. Every construction has to be specified and tested per chain (see the clock convention in §3.3).

5. Threshold key lifecycle. Distributed key generation and refresh as arbitrators join and leave, and how those operations interact with cases that are already open.

6. Signing at acceptance. Appendix B drafts the outputs, transactions and message formats for the pre-signed set (§3), the bucket sizes for continuous splits, and the delegate rules. What it still needs is per-chain test vectors, benchmarks of how long a phone takes to produce the payout set, and adversarial testing. Two residuals are accepted rather than solved: an incident where no caller-side holder can sign before acceptance proceeds without escrow protection, and every accepted incident costs one on-chain transaction; a disputed case settles in up to three payout transactions, one per pot.

Acceptance criterion. These constructions count as solved when they have been implemented on testnet and exercised adversarially — every path terminates, no funds can be trapped, no party can spend unilaterally, behaviour under signer dropout is defined — not when they have been described.

Fallback. If threshold-attested verdict release cannot be made to meet these criteria, the design degrades to pre-accepted third-party arbitrators holding federated multisig custody — the opt-in path of §10.9 generalised. That is simpler and easier to audit, but if the arbitrators are identifiable it is less resistant to compulsion. The pseudonymous pool is the harder version of the problem and the one this protocol is designed to solve.

Open issues found in the v6 review. The items below were found while splitting v5.44 into the three v6 documents. They are design questions, not drafting slips, and are recorded here rather than resolved silently.

7. Arbitration fees and the pre-signed set. §11.11 says fees are paid “from the incident output when the panel sits” and settled by deducting them from the caller’s returned deposit or reimbursing them from a responder’s bond. Each incident output is a key-path output spent exactly once (§3.1), and at acceptance neither the panel’s members nor the fee (§10.6) is known, so neither the mid-case payment nor a settlement amount can be pre-signed. A candidate fix: the escalating party prepays the panel fee from its own funds when the panel sits, as appellants already do (§10.5), and the pre-signed set carries only a fee shift of an amount fixed at acceptance, such as the first-instance fee quoted at the acceptance height; re-roll costs stay with the appellant and the appeal subsidy. The partitioned escrow (§3.5) would then hold that fixed amount.

8. Non-revealed votes. A late reveal is treated as an abstention with no penalty (§10.4). Reveals are not simultaneous, so an arbitrator who sees the revealed votes going against them can withhold their own and escape the asymmetric penalty, making it optional. A candidate fix: a committed vote that is not revealed in time is charged as an incoherent vote at the isolation penalty, and counts toward momentum.

9. Insurer buffer custody. Appendix B.1 gives the insurer buffer output the insurer’s wallet key as its key path, so the insurer can withdraw layer 0 at any time, and the forfeiture of layer 0 and the drawing of fines from it “first” (§11.8, §4) cannot be enforced. A candidate fix: build a buffer like a bond output (insurer’s scoped key plus the oracle’s co-signature, forfeitures pre-signed at the client’s exposure, a long fallback), and, for payment-backing buffers, add the same no-open-alert withdrawal rule as the caller’s funding output.

10. Fraud histories backed by one bond — resolved in v6.0. Stake above exposure makes a single act of cheating unprofitable once the chance of review passes gain over stake, but a history of n cheats backed by one bond gains on every case and loses the bond once. v6.0 adds cascade review (§10.3): a confirmed fraud reopens every case of the identities involved within a lookback window, with restitution case by case, and raises the review rate on cases in their cells for a period. Still open: the lookback window, the rate increase and its duration, which need simulation.

11. Footage access consent. C6.4 (Appendix C) and §11.10 promise that footage is accessible only to the caller and attendees by default, that third parties need a signed consent transaction, and that every access is recorded on-chain. BCAEN is content-addressed and “retrievable by anyone with the hash” (§14.1), and access events are not among the classes committed on-chain (§7.2). Either footage is encrypted at upload to a per-case key released by consent (to the parties, and to panels and reviewers on assignment), or the consent and logging claims are withdrawn.

12. The formal arbitration leaf. §10.9 describes the federation as “a fourth Taproot spending path … alongside the cooperative, verdict, and timeout paths”, but the incident outputs are MuSig2 key-path outputs with no script tree (Appendix B.1). An opted-in escrow would need a federation leaf on each incident output, or the federation would act as the oracle for that escrow.

13. The position on nation-state attackers. The threat map (Appendix C, header and C4.6, C8.3) presents formal arbitration as the escalation for nation-state threats and says the protocol tier is not designed to survive them alone; §10.9 says formal arbitration is not the nation-state defence and that the protocol is self-defending against state-level attack. One position has to be chosen.

14. Sponsorship. Sponsorship (Foundations §5, §12, C7.2) lets a caller extend their deposit as coverage for invitees, seized if any invitee misuses. No output, payout or forfeiture in Appendix B implements it, and the payout set of §3.1 has no class for it. It needs a construction (for example, a sponsor buffer with layer 0 exposure) or removal.

15. Price-out and the escalating party. Price-out ejection (§10.7) tests the caller’s deposit against the current fee, but a dispute may be escalated by a responder over classification; a well-funded responder’s dispute could be ejected because the caller’s deposit is small. This resolves together with item 7.

16. Undefined terms in the merit weight. The merit-weight formulas (§9, Appendix A) use bounty_factor and labor_factor without defining them.

Appendix A — Protocol Parameter Reference

Parameters marked deflating follow the 20% annual nominal schedule computed from Bitaid’s application-layer genesis block height. Parameters marked calibrated are set at genesis and adjustable only through the governance-free fork mechanism (§7). Block counts for protocol windows are Bitcoin block heights (~10 minutes per block), the clock of the anchored event log (§7); script timelocks are stated in wall-clock time with a block count per chain (§3.3).

Which parameters bear the load. Not every number below matters equally. The security argument depends on a few: the ⅔ labor-qualified invariant, the stake-above-exposure gate, 90-day seasoning, the roughly 97% break-even set by the penalty and momentum rules, the review sampling rate, and the 80% eligibility floor. Changing one of these changes what an attack costs, and each should move only with simulation behind it. Others are expected to be found by the market and can be tuned freely: fee levels, outcome bucket sizes, offer sizes, the intervener/documenter split, and insurer layer pricing. The rest are operational windows and floors. A machine-readable copy of this table (the parameter file) accompanies the paper, so that simulations and the reference implementation load the same values.

Bonds & Floors

Entry cost tracks the liability each role assumes. A caller risks their deposit against a misuse ruling. A responder risks their bond against misuse and bad-faith arbitration, with their physical conduct on camera and subject to arbitration review. An arbitrator risks 2× to 4× the per-case fee, deducted from their bond, on each incoherent vote, compounding up to 32× when errors cluster. An archival node risks proportional forfeiture against a confirmed failure to serve retrieval requests. The ratios are deliberate: caller at 5,000 sat, responder at 0.01 BTC (200×), arbitrator at 0.1 BTC (2,000×), archival at 0.005 BTC (100×). The caller floor deflates at 20% annually to preserve accessibility regardless of Bitcoin’s price trajectory; service-provider floors carry no deflation schedule, so Bitcoin appreciation passively filters toward professionalisation on the supply side.

Arbitrator pool Entry

The pool is closed by default with a target size equal to the rolling median of active arbitrators over 200 case-event windows. Slots open only on natural turnover (exit, ejection, forfeiture) or sustained demand signal (72 hours of non-zero queue after price-out ejection). Entry operates on three tracks depending on calibration library maturity. Track A (coherence competition, library ≥ 100 cases) requires applicants to answer 15 cases — 10 live shadow votes and 5 canon cases — pay a non-refundable BCAEN participation fee, and lock a scaled intention bond. Track B (invitation) lets proven arbitrators vouch for candidates. Track C (weighted lottery) is a fallback for a launch whose canon holds fewer than 100 cases; under the planned launch it is dormant. The 100-case threshold for Track A activation balances the need for a meaningful calibration library against the risk of prolonged lottery-based entry during genesis.

Arbitration mechanism

The ⅔ labor-qualified invariant is the core Sybil defence at the panel level: it guarantees that no panel can be majority-Sybil regardless of how many fake identities enter the pool. The 80% coherence threshold is the eligibility floor; with recurrence-weighted penalties, serving pays only above about 97% coherence. Configurable quality settings (coherence threshold, open seat fraction, bond minimum) create a tiered premium service market for adjudication quality. The bond minimum decays at ×0.70 per 144-block interval to prevent deadlock when high settings cannot fill a panel.

Fee Market

The dual-trigger mechanism adapts EIP-1559 with a time-based congestion trigger that fires even when no cases are closing. The asymmetry is deliberate: fees rise from both case closes and time during congestion, but fall only on successful case clears. By 72 hours of continuous congestion the time trigger alone multiplies the fee by about 4.3× (1.0625^24); each case close while the queue is non-zero adds a further 12.5%, so with ten closes in that window the fee is about 14× its starting level. There is no protocol-imposed floor; the market clears wherever supply and demand meet. The 36-block price-out grace window ensures queue depth signals are clean: every case in the queue at block H is fundable at fee(H).

Forfeiture Flows

All forfeiture events route through a consistent framework: panel fees first at normal case rate, then type-specific remainder destinations. No forfeiture event enriches the ruling panel beyond their standard per-case fee — this closes the captured-panel attack where a majority might falsely condemn honest participants to extract seized bonds.

Network & Routing

Dead-end scoring enforces liveness without a relay bond: nodes that fail to propagate see reduced routing priority and eventual exclusion. The thresholds (0.3 for deprioritisation, 0.6 for exclusion) use rolling windows and count only failures after a signed receipt, so a network split is not scored and an excluded node can recover through probation. Grid cells at H3 resolution 7 (~5 km², ~1.4 km edge) balance geographic precision against propagation overhead.

Evidence (BCAEN)

Chunk size at 256 KB provides standard-definition footage intervals. The 90-day BCAEN subsidy drip period releases retroactive forfeiture gradually to avoid market shock while attracting new archival nodes through temporarily elevated yields.

B.3 The oracle key and per-case nonces

Membership. Arbitrators who have completed probation and seasoning. Threshold t = ⌊n/2⌋ + 1 of the n members at each epoch. An epoch is 2,016 Bitcoin blocks (about two weeks, provisional); membership changes take effect at epoch boundaries.

Key. Generated once by distributed key generation (the FROST key generation procedure), then proactively reshared at every epoch boundary so that departing members’ shares become useless while the public key P stays the same (D-FROST is the starting point).

Nonces. At each epoch the members jointly generate a batch of one-time secrets k_j, each Shamir-shared like the key, and publish the points R_j = k_j·G, negating k_j where needed so that R_j has an even y-coordinate as BIP340 requires. Batch size is set well above the busiest epoch observed (provisionally 10× peak), because a spare nonce costs each member only one stored share. When half the batch has been assigned, the members generate the next batch at once rather than waiting for the epoch boundary. A case accepted while no nonce is free waits in the queue until the next batch arrives — minutes in normal operation — and never proceeds without one; cases already open are unaffected. Unused nonces of open cases are reshared with the key at the epoch boundary; nonces never assigned are discarded.

Assignment. When a case is accepted it takes the next unused index for each of its pots — three indices j, recorded in the anchored log. For each outcome m, anyone can compute the attestation point S_m = R_j + e_m·P, where e_m is the BIP340 challenge hash of (R_j, P, m). These are the points the payouts’ adaptor signatures are encrypted to.

Attestation. After the last re-roll window closes, each member replays the anchored log, checks the outcome, that the three pots’ outcomes agree (the reward’s split is the bounty’s), and that the case’s payout sets match the caller’s committed template, and releases, for each pot, its partial s_i = k_{j,i} + e_m·λ_i·x_i. Any t valid partials, each checked against the member’s public shares, combine into s, and (R_j, s) is a BIP340 signature on m under P. A member never releases a partial for index j on a second message; an index is spent once its attestation is posted. Signing a second message with the same nonce would reveal the key.

Liveness. Partials are collected asynchronously in the manner of ROAST, so absent members do not stall a case once t have responded. If t cannot be reached, the case waits; the incident refund is the backstop.

What a captured majority can do. With t shares of the key and of the nonces, a coalition can attest any outcome for any case: it can push wrong verdicts among the pre-signed payouts, and co-sign early bond withdrawals for operators who also sign. It cannot create a payout no party signed. The oracle’s power is therefore bounded settlement authority: it chooses which pre-signed outcome becomes executable, never what the outcomes are. A wrong attestation is visible — anyone replaying the anchored log can see that the attested outcome does not follow from the recorded votes — but a threshold signature does not reveal which members signed, so there is no individual slashing for it. The remedy is pool-wide: a fork and a fresh key. The same holds for withdrawal approvals, where a captured majority could delay honest withdrawals up to the 365-day fallback. That is the condition whose cost §10.4 and Foundations §6.2 make the protocol’s central defence.

B.4 Scoped keys and signing delegates

Authorisation. At registration the caller’s wallet signs a message binding the scoped key K to the funding outpoints and to the template hash T. T commits to every caller-side output — the refund address and anything else owed to the caller — and to the rule that the incident transaction’s only non-anchor outputs are the incident outputs for the responders recorded in the log.

Holders. The caller chooses up to three holders: the phone, the caller’s Core, or bonded signing delegates. Any one of them can sign; the first valid signature package wins.

Delegates. A delegate is a bonded BCAEN node whose bond exceeds the total value of the funding outputs delegated to it (§2.3). At delegation it pre-signs a forfeiture of that bond to the caller. Any spend of a funding output through the scoped-key leaf that is not the template’s incident transaction is deterministic evidence; the oracle then co-signs the delegate’s forfeiture. Delegates earn a fee per incident signed.

B.5 Availability verification

  1. A retriever whose authenticated retrieval fails posts an availability challenge with a small challenge bond.
  2. Five arbitrators are drawn by a block hash mined after the challenge. Within 6 blocks each sends ordinary retrieval requests for the chunk over I2P, indistinguishable from any other retrieval, and records the result by commit-reveal.
  3. If at least four of the five fail to retrieve the chunk, the node’s base-slot availability deposit is forfeited by the pre-signed forfeiture (§12), the challenge bond is returned, and the probers are paid from the deposit. Otherwise the challenge fails, the challenge bond pays the probers, and the node is unaffected.

Because the content is addressed by hash, a single successful retrieval proves availability at that moment. Because probes look like ordinary retrievals, a node cannot serve only the probers. Three rules keep the check from becoming a griefing tool: the five probers are drawn from different regions, so a local outage or network partition cannot make an honest node look absent; a node that fails a check gets a recovery window of 6 blocks to serve the chunk before forfeiture; and repeated challenges against the same node within 144 blocks require doubling challenge bonds.

B.8 Minimum viable path

A first implementation does not need the whole protocol. The smallest loop that runs real incidents end to end is:

  • Registration: the funding output, the scoped key and the committed caller template (§3, B.4).
  • Alert: the geofenced alert with its economic offer (§7).
  • Acceptance: the incident transaction and the pre-signed refund, with a single payout per responder share and no oracle yet (§3).
  • Evidence: hash-before-upload to a small set of archival nodes (§8).
  • Settlement: the attending vote and cooperative close (§9).

That loop already delivers the core promise: a bonded responder answers a bonded alert, the evidence is pinned, and payment settles by agreement or by refund. Everything else is added in layers, each testable on its own: the oracle and pre-signed outcome payouts (B.1, B.3); arbitration, re-rolls and the challenge bond (§10.4–10.5); review sampling and momentum (§10.3–10.4); insurer buffer stacks (§4); Track B entry. Building in this order means the first incidents test the parts every later layer depends on. Before any checklist settles real funds, a mechanical test suite should run every allowed combination of predicate answers through the rules and confirm that each yields exactly one payout from the pre-signed set, one bond consequence and one labor update, rejecting any combination that yields none or several.

Appendix C — Threat Vector Map

Protocol-layer outcomes. Failed commercial abuse typically forfeits bonds to honest participants and raises local acceptance thresholds. This is not chain re-org or rule change. Tail risk: insurer mispricing (off-protocol).

Open issue (§16, item 13): this map treats formal arbitration as the escalation for nation-state threats, while §10.9 states that formal arbitration is not the nation-state defence. The two positions are kept as written until one is chosen.

Adversary Model

The protocol is calibrated for commercially-motivated adversaries : opportunistic individuals, small Sybil farms, capital floods, and patient reputation farmers. For disputes where a nation-state actor is a plausible threat, the voluntary formal arbitration path (§10.9) is the correct escalation — the protocol tier is not designed to survive well-resourced state attacks alone.

1. CALLER-SIDE ATTACKS — Troll alerts, false incidents, zero-cost exits, low-signal stakes

C1.1 · §2.2, §9, §11 — Troll / false alert

  • Attack: Caller triggers alert with no real emergency — wastes responder time, farms BCAEN footage, denies service to others in the same grid cell.
  • Primary defence — Stake forfeiture on misuse ruling: Caller’s deposit seized on a panel finding (§11); attendees may flag misuse but not decide it, and responders who find no incident can fast-track the case (§9). The deposit is locked at acceptance and the pre-signed payout completes on the oracle’s attestation — no court required. Cost per troll alert = stake amount.
  • Secondary defence — Labor + sponsorship revocation: Confirmed troll rating raises every local responder’s acceptance threshold against the caller. Sponsorship chain (Foundations §5) is revoked: all dependents lose coverage. Insurer drops coverage on conviction.
  • Residual / limit — One-shot attacks at genesis floor: Minimum 5,000 sat stake may be below attacker’s cost threshold for a single attack. Mitigated by local operator acceptance thresholds (§2.2) which adapt per cell.

C1.2 · §3, §9, §11 — Non-cooperation refund exit

  • Attack: Caller refuses to co-sign after getting service, waits out the refund locktime to reclaim stake, obtaining intervention for free.
  • Primary defence — Attending-only vote (load-bearing): Merit-weighted verdict by physically-present responders overrides caller non-cooperation. An 80% vote with no escalation closes by deemed consent: the oracle attests it and the matching pre-signed payout completes (§9). At acceptance the deposit moves into incident outputs the caller cannot leave alone, so no exit is possible mid-ruling (§3).
  • Secondary defence — BCAEN footage evidence: Multi-source, hash-pinned attendance and outcome evidence makes caller’s denial contradict the documentary record at arbitration.
  • Residual / limit — Low-attendance incidents: If only one responder attends, merit weight concentrates on a single vote. Caller contesting may escalate to arbitration tier (§10) — adds latency and cost but resolves.

C1.3 · §5.1, §14.3 — Caller recovery address hijack

  • Attack: Attacker compromises caller device or social-engineers the designated recovery address, diverting stake return on incapacitation.
  • Primary defence — User key hygiene · caller responsibility: Recovery address is user-chosen at registration and may be a hardware wallet or multisig address. Signed disclosure required — no silent change.
  • Residual / limit — RESIDUAL RISK: Key management is the caller’s responsibility. Compromised device → compromised recovery. Protocol cannot mitigate endpoint security.

C1.4 · §2.2, §2.3, §4 — Sub-threshold stake signal

  • Attack: Caller posts only the genesis minimum, sending a weak credibility signal. Not malicious — a market outcome.
  • Primary defence — Per-operator acceptance thresholds: Each Core operator sets local threshold above the protocol floor. Rolling local median published as advisory. Market clears — no enforcement required.
  • Residual / limit — Insurance as access floor: Insurer-backed stakes (§4) enable subscribers below personal-capital threshold to access meaningful signalling.

2. RESPONDER-SIDE ATTACKS — Phantom attendance, vote collusion, witness free-riding, bad-faith intervention

C2.1 · §8, §11 — Phantom attendance

  • Attack: Responder claims attendance remotely with fabricated GPS metadata and synthetic or replayed footage, claiming bounty without showing up.
  • Primary defence — BCAEN multi-source cross-validation: Presence must be shown in BCAEN metadata and footage. Hash pre-propagated before upload — fabrication must be consistent across N independent recorders whose hashes are already in gossip.
  • Secondary defence — Ballot fraud + bond forfeiture: A false claim of presence or credit is fraud under C2.1 Q5: bannable, with the bond and first insurer layer seized. Labor is scored per cell (§6), so it cannot be escaped by relocating.
  • Residual / limit — Hardware attestation gap: Non-attested GPS modules admit spoofing. Mitigation: insurers require attested hardware for coverage (§14.3) — market pressure toward adoption without protocol-level enforcement.

C2.2 · §9, §11 — Attending-vote collusion

  • Attack: Multiple responders collude to vote against a legitimate caller — deny bounty, assert false outcome, redirect reward to themselves.
  • Primary defence — Arbitration tier escalation: Caller escalates contested vote to independent arbitrators. BCAEN footage is primary documentary evidence. Colluding responders risk bond and reputation on false assertion.
  • Secondary defence — Supermajority threshold (§9): Merit-weighted supermajority required. Top-weight attendees alone cannot decide — tiebreaker role for lower-weight attendees prevents capital-only dominance.
  • Residual / limit — Unanimous-attendee collusion: If every attendee is colluding, the attending vote is captured. BCAEN footage from the caller’s own device and any admissible outside sources enables arbitration tier to override attending verdict.

C2.3 · §9, §11 — Witness free-rider capture

  • Attack: Low-bond witnesses outnumber actual interveners, vote in attending tier to deny rewards to those who acted and claim bounty for themselves.
  • Primary defence — Weight by bond × labor, not count: weight = sqrt(stake_ratio) × (0.1 + labor_factor × bounty_factor). Witnesses without significant bond and labor history carry marginal weight — head-count alone cannot capture.
  • Secondary defence — Pay follows recorded acts: Pay comes from the acts each attendee is shown doing (C2.3): interveners from the 70% pool, documenters from the 30%. Presence without contribution earns nothing, however many ballots it casts.
  • Residual / limit — Witnesses as evidence layer, not voters: Emergent witness role flows through BCAEN footage as evidence, not through governance votes with meaningful weight. Closes the free-rider vector without formalising a witness role.

C2.4 · §11, §14.4 — Excessive-force intervention

  • Attack: Responder uses excessive force or intervenes beyond jurisdiction’s legal limit for citizen arrest — causes harm, exposes caller to liability. When caller and responders partially collude (C1.2, C2.2, C2.3), the attending-only vote can be captured — but coherent BCAEN evidence from attendees and third parties is the arbitrator’s leverage. The arbitrators rule on the documentary record, not on claims. Forged accounts cannot survive adversarial review of what the footage actually shows. This is why the evidence layer ( §8 ) and the arbitration layer ( §10 ) are designed together: the first produces incontestable record, the second adjudicates from it.
  • Primary defence — Bond forfeiture + arbitrator sanction: BCAEN footage documents the intervention itself. The panel answers the C2.4 predicates and the tier table sets the penalty. Formal arbitration path for civil/criminal liability (§10.9).
  • Residual / limit — RESIDUAL RISK: Protocol does not replace criminal law. Responder remains subject to local prosecution. Jurisdiction-specific legal reference surfaced to responder app (§5.2) but is advisory only.

3. SYBIL ATTACKS — Identity flooding, reputation farming, cross-role escalation, geographic laundering

C3.1 · §2.2, §10.7 — Bulk caller Sybil farm

  • Attack: Thousands of caller identities created to flood alerts, exhaust responder attention, manufacture reputation for later deployment.
  • Primary defence — Stake lockup per identity + tx fees: Every identity requires its own stake UTXO + non-recoverable registration fee. Cost scales linearly with identity count regardless of attack success. N identities = N× capital + N× fees, compounding.
  • Secondary defence — Queue weight by responder legitimacy (§10.7): Cases attended only by unseasoned, low-stake identities add negligible queue weight, so manufactured accounts cannot force arbitration slots open.
  • Residual / limit — Geofenced gossip bounds blast radius: Sybil alerts in one cell do not flood distant cells. Local operators can raise thresholds during flooding events. Local cleanup, not global impact.

C3.2 · §6, §10.3 — Cross-role labor transfer

  • Attack: Build cheap responder labor on low-stake calls, then exercise it to influence arbitrator panels or earn large bounties in other roles.
  • Primary defence — Role-locked labor (per-role): Responder reputation ≠ arbitrator eligibility. Arbitrator coherence ≠ responder weight. Caller clean history ≠ vote weight. Each role: independent bond, independent case history, independent seasoning.
  • Residual / limit — Three separate expensive operations: Farm must execute in parallel in each role it wants to compromise. Breaks a whole class of escalation attacks present in “universal reputation” designs.

C3.3 · §6, §10.3 — Geographic labor laundering

  • Attack: Build responder reputation cheaply in low-activity permissive cell, then exercise that weight in high-value disputes in a distant active cell.
  • Primary defence — Grid-cell-scoped responder reputation: Responder reputation accumulates within the grid cells where incidents occurred. Cell A history does not transfer to cell B disputes. Attacker must farm in each target cell independently.
  • Residual / limit — Arbitrator reputation is deliberately global: Arbitrator coherence draws on network-wide case history — and arbitrators are spread globally, unlike the local parties to an incident, so collusion is not local. Two reputations, non-transferable.

4. ARBITRATOR ATTACKS — The hardest surface — pool flooding, verdict corruption, patient infiltration, appeal abuse

C4.1 · §10.2, §10.3 — Capital flood — buy majority

  • Attack: Attacker posts very large arbitrator bonds to dominate panel selection by raw bond weight, corrupting verdicts they are selected for.
  • Primary defence — Closed pool with merit-based entry: Pool closed by default. Entry requires winning a slot via Track A (coherence competition + non-refundable BCAEN fee), Track B (invitation from 50-case/90% arbitrator), or Track C (lottery). Capital cannot guarantee entry; work determines selection.
  • Secondary defence — Blind taking + stake above exposure: Seats are taken blind, ties drawn by block hash. Each arbitrator’s own bond must exceed the case value. ⅔ labor-qualified invariant is fixed. Open ⅓ configurable to 0. Bond minimum rises, never below the case value.
  • Residual / limit — Pricing inversion is the deterrent: Arbitrators are paid when right and lose 2–4× the fee when wrong, compounding up to 32× for a pattern. A capital flood faces bond erosion if it votes against the honest labor-qualified majority.

C4.2 · §10.3, §10.8 — Patient labor farming

  • Attack: Attacker creates arbitrator accounts and votes honestly for months to reach labor-qualified status, then deploys for targeted verdict corruption.
  • Primary defence — Closed pool + slot competition (§10.3): Pool closed by default. Entry requires winning a slot via Track A (coherence competition + non-refundable BCAEN fee per identity) or Track B invitation. 1,000 Sybils = 1,000 non-refundable fees.
  • Secondary defence — Seasoning + 90-day exit delay (§10.3, §10.8): labor_score × min(1, days/90) discount. Rapidly-built history down-weighted. Voluntary exit: mandatory 90-day hold. Active fraud review extends hold. Bond present to seize — cannot farm and exit.
  • Residual / limit — Mechanism converts attacker into arbitrator: An attacker who votes honestly long enough to pass calibration IS voting honestly. The only way to deploy is to betray calibration, which immediately removes them.

C4.3 · §10.2, §10.4 — Coordinated voting bloc

  • Attack: Multiple accounts controlled by one operator vote identically on contested cases, appear labor-qualified from manufactured correlated history.
  • Primary defence — Closed pool + calibration injection: Pool closed by default — bloc must win slots via Track A competition against calibration cases (§10.3). Accounts that collude on real cases must also score honestly on calibration to enter.
  • Residual / limit — Coordinated dissent is self-punishing: Each dissent from the bloc exposes the attacker to the incoherence penalty (2–4× the fee, compounding up to 32×). Voting together risks detection via calibration case scoring (§10.3).

C4.4 · §10.4, §10.6 — Fee-market capture (low-bidder)

  • Attack: Attacker bids zero or minimal fees to win every arbitrator seat on price, driving out honest arbitrators with real opportunity costs.
  • Primary defence — Penalty market inversion: There is no bid-ask market for arbitrator seats. Arbitrators ARE paid normal per-case fees when coherent — and PENALISED 2–4× when incoherent, compounding up to 32× for a pattern. Inverts the capture logic.
  • Residual / limit — Core insight, protected by layers: The asymmetric incentive only holds if the honest majority is present, uncor-related, and not outnumbered — which is what C4.1, C4.2, C4.3 collectively ensure. The layers protect the alignment.

C4.5 · §10.3, §12 — Fabricated incident chain

  • Attack: Attacker controls caller + responders + complicit arbitrators to manufacture a fake incident and extract funds from the escrow through apparently legitimate arbitration.
  • Primary defence — Automatic retroactive detection: 40% dissent → flag for urgent re-review. 100-arbitrator review panel, 80% supermajority for confirmed fabrication. All stakes in chain forfeited. Harmed party repaid first; then panel fees, BCAEN subsidy.
  • Secondary defence — Passive statistical detection: Each closed case is drawn for review with probability R (5%+, by value); 40% dissent → flag; 80% on the 100-arbitrator review. No individual submitter needed. Detection compounds: 79% after 30 fake cases.
  • Residual / limit — Time becomes attacker’s liability: Each fake case may later be reviewed via calibration injection. The farm cannot predict which cases or when. Forfeiture pool grows with operation size. Patience flips into vulnerability.

C4.6 · §10.4, §10.5 — Appeal inflation / DoS by escalation

  • Attack: Losing party appeals repeatedly to drain opponent’s resources via bond contribution required per appeal round, or to fish for a favourable panel.
  • Primary defence — Escalating appeal fees + panel size: Appeals are paid re-rolls, +25% per tier. Re-rolls: 3 → 7 → 15 → 31 → … Each round costs more in fees and arbitration time than the last. Self-deterring against frivolous escalation.
  • Residual / limit — Deep-pocket attacker can still escalate: For sufficiently well-funded adversaries, escalation cost is tolerable. Uncapped re-rolls at ⅔ supermajority reach ever larger, less pollutable panels. Formal arbitration path (§10.9) as final escalation.

C4.7 · §4, §10.2, §10.3 — Self-sponsored Sybil arbitrator

  • Attack: Attacker backs their own Sybil arbitrators with buffers from shell entities, hoping capital buys seats.
  • Primary defence — Capital buys no seats: Buffers carry zero weight; seats are taken blind and drawn by block hash. Fines draw the buffer first, and each arbitrator’s own bond must exceed the case.
  • Residual / limit — Entry is by competition, not capital: Each Sybil must still win a coherence competition against the canon, serve probation and season for 90 days. Capital only adds what the attacker can lose.

5. NETWORK & INFRASTRUCTURE ATTACKS — Gossip forgery, eclipse, GPS spoofing, relay subversion, dead-end abuse

C5.1 · §7 — Gossip / alert forgery

  • Attack: Malicious relay injects fake alerts into gossip layer without a valid caller stake UTXO or signature — flooding peers, manufacturing incidents.
  • Primary defence — Cryptographic alert signing: Every alert signed by caller private key and cryptographically linked to live stake UTXO. Any receiving Core verifies before acting. Forgery fails at first honest Core regardless of relay count.
  • Residual / limit — Low-cost — first honest peer filters: Verification is fast, local, and deterministic. Forged alerts do not propagate past their originating relay. Persistent forgers accumulate dead-end score and are ejected.

C5.2 · §5.3, §7 — Eclipse attack on caller

  • Attack: Attacker surrounds caller’s Core with malicious peers, preventing honest alert propagation, intercepting metadata, or feeding false network state.
  • Primary defence — Tor routing + Kademlia diversity: All connections over Tor Hidden Services — attacker cannot identify caller IP. Kademlia DHT provides peer diversity; identity derived from keypair makes targeted sybil peer cluster difficult.
  • Secondary defence — Dead-end scoring ejects silent peers: Cores that observe no onward propagation from a peer attest to that observation. Accumulating dead-end attestations → deprioritisation and eventual exclusion. Self-healing over time.
  • Residual / limit — Targeted pre-incident eclipse: A short-window eclipse at the moment an alert triggers could suppress a single incident. 911 fallback call mitigates by providing a non-protocol channel to emergency services (§5.1).

C5.3 · §5.3, §7 — Dead-end attestation abuse

  • Attack: Malicious peers falsely attest honest nodes as dead-ends to trigger their ejection from peer routing tables, partitioning the network.
  • Primary defence — Attestations are themselves peer-observable: Cores that consistently attest against peers who provably relayed (verifiable by other peers’ observations) become themselves flagged for divergent attestation — their weight on subsequent attestations diminishes.
  • Residual / limit — Attestation is advisory, not authoritative: Dead-end score is per-peer heuristic weighting, not global ban. A node flagged by some peers may still serve others. Partitioning attacks require sustained majority control of local peer observers.

C5.4 · §8, §14.3 — GPS spoofing

  • Attack: Caller or responder falsifies GPS coordinates to claim attendance at a remote incident, manufacture a false incident location, or spoof proximity.
  • Primary defence — Multi-source cross-validation: Multiple independent recorders at same scene. Consistent fabrication across N independent devices, angles, and GPS units scales in difficulty with attendance. Structural — hash pre-propagated before coordination.
  • Secondary defence — Hardware attestation market pressure: Signed-GPS modules + tamper-evident body cameras. Insurers requiring attested hardware for coverage create market adoption pressure without protocol-level enforcement.
  • Residual / limit — Low-attendance incidents (residual): Single-responder incidents lack cross-validation. Farm cost (bond, tx fees, Tor identity maintenance) likely exceeds small bounty at scale. Will need empirical monitoring as deflation schedule lowers floor.

C5.5 · §7 — Bootstrap node subversion

  • Attack: Attacker compromises hardcoded bootstrap nodes to partition network, feed false peer lists, or isolate new nodes.
  • Primary defence — Replaceable bootstrap config: Bootstrap list hardcoded but replaceable via config. No single bootstrap is SPoF. Kademlia DHT self-heals partitions once any honest peer is found.
  • Residual / limit — Cold-start assumption: New nodes assume at least one honest bootstrap. Config distribution is the governance problem outside the protocol layer.

C5.6 · §13.2 — Payment rail compromise (Boltz)

  • Attack: Boltz service or Lightning path is compromised / censored / malicious, blocking caller/responder funding or withdrawal flows.
  • Primary defence — Submarine swaps are non-custodial: Swap linked by hash preimage — no custodian holds funds at any point. Boltz can censor service but cannot steal. Alternative rails (direct L-BTC, on-chain Bitcoin) remain available.
  • Residual / limit — Service dependency, not trust: Boltz outage degrades UX for Lightning-only users. Protocol remains functional via Liquid or Bitcoin L1 direct. Multi-provider swap market reduces single-point dependency over time.

6. EVIDENCE & BCAEN ATTACKS — Footage suppression, availability fraud, deepfakes, access leakage, slot collusion, malicious content injection

C6.1 · §8, §8.9 — Footage suppression

  • Attack: Attacker intercepts or deletes footage before distribution to remove evidence of misconduct or incident.
  • Primary defence — Hash-first gossip propagation: SHA-256 hash broadcast over gossip BEFORE upload completes. Integrity fingerprint already distributed to hundreds of nodes before any single copy lands anywhere deleteable.
  • Residual / limit — Hash without content: Destroying all redundant copies leaves the hash but not the footage. Archival node redundancy target (§8.3) makes total loss require compromising every selected slot winner.

C6.2 · §8.3, §8.5 — Archival availability fraud

  • Attack: Archival node wins slot, collects early payment tranches, then fails to produce footage on later challenges or deletes selectively.
  • Primary defence — Tiered redundancy + demand-driven enforcement: Base slots (3-5) labor-gated; marginal 6+ open market. Failure is confirmed by probes from several regions with a 6-block recovery window (B.5). Self-backup = 3rd layer. Evidence survives at 3 tiers.
  • Residual / limit — Coordinated redundancy failure: All N selected archivers colluding to fail together is possible but all lose their bonds simultaneously. Cost to attacker: entire redundancy set’s bond sum plus availability score damage.

C6.3 · §8, §11 — AI deepfake substitution

  • Attack: Attacker replaces genuine BCAEN footage with AI-generated video showing a different or false outcome at the same scene.
  • Primary defence — Content-addressed integrity: SHA-256 hash pre-propagated to gossip before any coordination is possible. Any substituted file produces a different hash — detectable by any node. Signed by recorder’s private key.
  • Secondary defence — Multi-angle structural corroboration: N independent recorders with N different angles make consistent fabrication across all feeds scale in difficulty with attendance — structural resistance to generative attacks.
  • Residual / limit — Single-source incidents: Incidents with only one recorder lack structural corroboration. Hardware attestation raises the bar significantly (signed chip output). Residual risk on low-attendance low-value incidents.

C6.4 · §11, §14.1 — Unauthorised footage access

  • Attack: Attacker attempts to obtain BCAEN footage without signed consent — state coercion, infrastructure compromise, social engineering.
  • Primary defence — Consent-required access + on-chain audit: Default: only caller and attendees can access their incident footage. Third-party access requires signed consent transaction. Every access event recorded on-chain for audit.
  • Residual / limit — RESIDUAL RISK: Protocol does not block court-ordered access — it ensures access is recorded. Key custody by consenting party remains subject to coercion. Consent-sig disclosure is the final gate.

C6.5 · §8.3, §8.4 — Archival slot auction collusion

  • Attack: Archival nodes collude to submit floor-price bids, exclude honest bidders, or share retrieval workload below the redundancy target.
  • Primary defence — Composite scoring + archival labor gate: Base slots: bids scored by price × labor score × availability × bond. Fresh nodes can only bid marginal slots 6+. Independent challenges verify each slot winner; no pooled retrieval possible.
  • Residual / limit — Market-based resistance: Honest archival node wins on track record. Colluding pool requires coordinating bond and availability history across identities, and every slot holder is checked independently by availability challenges (Appendix B.5).

C6.6 · §11, §14.4 — Malicious BCAEN content injection

  • Attack: Attacker uploads unrelated, copyrighted, or illegal content (worst case: CSAM) with forged incident metadata, weaponising BCAEN as a distribution channel or reputational attack on the protocol.
  • Primary defence — Metadata + fee + arbitration gauntlet: Upload requires valid stake UTXO and signed alert — metadata forgery is expensive, not free. Hosting micro-payments drain attacker stake per upload. Flagged content triggers arbitration review; ruling seizes stake and purges content.
  • Secondary defence — Redundancy slot release on ruling: Archival bond slots holding purged content are released; content removed from redundancy set across all archivers. Hash record persists as audit trail showing removal was properly adjudicated, not arbitrarily censored.
  • Residual / limit — Stronger than unregulated cloud: Every upload is stake-bonded, traceable to a signed incident, and subject to arbitration review with on-chain consequence. Rebuts the state-level objection that BCAEN could shelter illegal content — it censors such content more effectively than conventional storage.

7. ECONOMIC & MARKET ATTACKS — Insurer fraud, sponsorship cascade, protocol fork manipulation, fee-market gaming

C7.1 · §4 — Insurer over-commitment fraud

  • Attack: Insurer offers insured buffers exceeding their actual pool depth, banking on low simultaneous-incident probability. Cascade failure on correlated events.
  • Primary defence — Real-time encumbrance over gossip: Insurer UTXO encumbrance tracked and propagated on every covered alert. Callers and responders can verify the insurer’s live reserve ratio. Over-commitment is publicly visible.
  • Secondary defence — Market reputation + actuarial science: Insurers competing for subscribers are rated on their live reserve and historical payout record. Uncorrelated local incidents make actuarial estimates sound when honestly computed.
  • Residual / limit — Correlated disaster scenarios: Large-scale emergency (riot, disaster) breaks the uncorrelated assumption. Insurer buffer is seized first in order — damaged parties still receive direct caller stake. Loss ceiling bounded.

C7.2 · §12 — Sponsorship cascade exploit

Open issue (§16, item 14): sponsorship has no escrow construction yet. - Attack: Bad actor builds clean stake history, then sponsors many malicious invitees simultaneously to extract value before cascade revocation catches up. - Primary defence — Simultaneous cascade revocation: On any misuse ruling for a covered invitee, the covering stake is claimed AND all dependents lose coverage simultaneously. Sponsor loses capital; dependents lose access immediately. - Residual / limit — Pre-ruling extraction window: Sponsor may extract some value before first ruling lands. Vetting incentive is strong: sponsors price their risk and only extend coverage to invitees they trust.

C7.3 · §15 — Malicious protocol fork

  • Attack: Attacker promotes a fork with rule changes that weaken the Sybil defences, reduce bond penalties, or redirect forfeitures.
  • Primary defence — Weighted gossip fork signalling: Participants signal version preference in metadata on the protocol transactions they already make, weighted by bond and labor (§15). Both forks run simultaneously during transition. Market resolves which fork survives. On-chain bonds do not split.
  • Residual / limit — Positive-sum coordination: Bitaid forks are coordination disagreements, not monetary contests. Economic layer persists on Bitcoin regardless of application fork outcome.

C7.4 · §10.6, §10.7 — Fee market queue manipulation

  • Attack: Attacker manufactures dispute queue utilisation to force per-case fee up or down, pricing out honest arbitrators or suppressing fees unfairly.
  • Primary defence — Exponential fee burn + merit-gated slots: Dual-trigger compounding: +12.5%/close, +6.25%/18 blocks (~3h). Chessboard rice cost curve: 72h at ≥4.3× base fee (time trigger alone). Price-out ejection removes underfunded cases. Slashed bonds fund BCAEN pool + honest arbitrator fees.
  • Secondary defence — Slot opening requires earned history: 432-block trigger fires only after price-out proves genuine funded demand. New accounts with zero labor score can enter only by winning a Track A competition — fee, bond and exam per applicant — since Track B needs an invitation from a 50-case/90% arbitrator; Track C is dormant under the planned launch.
  • Residual / limit — Three parallel cost channels (§2.2): Fee escalation + per-node stake thresholds + bond seizure pipeline. Attacker faces three simultaneous capital drains; honest participant with labor history faces only the fee escalation. Channels are independent.

8. STATE & INSTITUTIONAL ATTACKS — Seizure, subpoena, nation-state verdict corruption — residual risks explicitly noted

C8.1 · §14.3 — On-chain bond seizure

  • Attack: State actor compels participant to surrender private keys under legal order or physical coercion.
  • Primary defence — Key custody outside Core daemon: Keys held by user device, never by Core. Compromised Core cannot redirect funds. Multisig recovery, hardware wallets, and dead-man switches are user choices.
  • Residual / limit — RESIDUAL RISK: Physical device seizure with key compromise succeeds. Protocol cannot resist physical coercion of the person. This is a human-rights limit, not a bug.

C8.2 · §7, §14.1 — Infrastructure subpoena

  • Attack: State demands logs, participant data, or alert metadata from a central server or service operator.
  • Primary defence — No central infrastructure: Protocol maintains no server, no database, no log store. All state distributed across participant nodes. IP obscured by Tor. Nothing to subpoena.
  • Residual / limit — Individual node subpoena possible: A specific archival node could be ordered to produce its slice. BCAEN consent-required access (C6.4) and on-chain audit of access events apply.

C8.3 · §10.2, §10.9 — Nation-state verdict corruption

  • Attack: State actor with vast resources attempts to corrupt arbitration verdicts on politically sensitive disputes through capital flood, identity infrastructure, or coercion of identified arbitrators.
  • Primary defence — Economic exhaustion at nation-state scale: Capital to corrupt a 31-arbitrator panel exceeds extractable value. All attacker capital is burned, not stolen — slashed bonds fund the BCAEN subsidy pool and honest arbitrator fees. Fee-inflation DoS is the only viable vector, and it only delays arbitration — never stops it.
  • Secondary defence — BCAEN evidence survives: Content-addressed evidence distributed before suppression is possible remains the documentary record regardless of which verdict forum resolves the dispute.
  • Residual / limit — RESIDUAL RISK: Nation-state with sufficient resources can corrupt the protocol-only tier. BCAEN + formal arbitration substantially raises attack cost. Singapore-seated arbitration backed by on-chain evidence presents a very different attack surface.

9. THE GAUNTLET — Sequential Attack Simulation — Step-by-step: what an attacker must survive to place one corrupted arbitrator in the pool

1. Attacker wants one pool slot

The pool is closed. Slots only open on natural turnover or sustained 432-block queue pressure.

  • TRAP: Closed pool with demand-signalled entry: Capital alone cannot open a slot. Demand signal required.

2. Must create queue pressure — 432 blocks

Queue depth is weighted by aggregate responder legitimacy and stake. Low-labor cases do not count. The queue clears as arbitrators pick up cases. Maintaining >0 requires dozens of fake high-labor responders, each 90-day seasoned, all in the same cell.

  • TRAP: Queue weight by responder legitimacy: One fake responder is not enough. Need a pipeline of seasoned fakes generating cases faster than arbitrators resolve them — all with real capital at risk, all cell-scoped, all under honest observation. For one slot.

3. Must build cell-scoped labor — in real cells

Labor score is recorded per H3 cell and does not transfer. Remote reputation farming is impossible.

  • TRAP: Cell-scoped labor: Must operate under honest participants’ continuous observation.

4. Must maintain deception for 90+ days

Every fake incident is permanent BCAEN evidence. Retroactive review at 40% dissent catches any slip.

  • TRAP: Temporal seasoning + retroactive review: 90-day minimum. Every case a permanent, growing liability.

5. Queue triggers slot open

Attacker enters coherence competition: must pay participation fee + intention bond + score well on 15 cases (10 live, 5 canon).

  • TRAP: Coherence competition: Work determines selection, not capital. Must outscore honest applicants.

6. Wins slot — enters 30-case probation

Votes recorded but do not count toward calibration. Seats capped at 1/3 of any panel — cannot control a verdict. 40% dissent on an old case flags for review by the 100-arbitrator pool.

  • TRAP: Probation with retroactive exposure: 40% flags; 100-pool confirms fraud at 80%. 3x premium from seized bond only.

7. Survives = 1 of ~200 arbitrators

Attacker now holds 1/n of the pool, where n grows over time. To corrupt a majority, repeat steps 1-6 (n/2+1) times.

  • RESULT: Compounding traps, not independent filters: Each “success” increases exposure. The defence accelerates.
